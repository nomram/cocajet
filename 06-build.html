Layer 1

The spine: shaft & bearing tunnel

Everything starts with the part that never moves relative to the rest and the part that spins faster than anything you own: a rigid tunnel carrying two bearings, and an 8 mm shaft running through it.

What it does

The bearing tunnel is a thick-walled steel tube bolted to the diffuser plate at the front and holding the shaft on two small ball bearings, one near each end. Everything else attaches to it. The shaft is a straight, hardened steel rod that locks the compressor wheel at one end to the turbine wheel at the other (an M8 nut at the hot end), so the turbine’s torque goes straight into the compressor.

The physics: rpm that nobody told the bearings about

Bearing makers rate speed with the DN number: bore diameter (mm) × rpm. Ours is $8\times115\,000=0.92\times10^6$, far beyond ordinary grease-packed bearings (~0.3 M). Hybrid ceramic-ball bearings with a little oil mist, or a turbocharger’s oil-fed journal bearings, can take it.

The second big constraint is the shaft’s critical speed: a spinning shaft has natural bending frequencies, and any imbalance excites them. Short, fat shafts have high criticals; long, thin ones low. Our engine’s first critical is ~40 000 rpm; we spend the first 10 seconds of every start passing through it, quickly, then run at 2.9× above it. That is only safe if the wheels are balanced (chapter 8).

Cheap ways to make it
  • Shaft: an 8 mm hardened, ground rod (a “linear shaft” or drill-rod, h6 tolerance) cut to ~168 mm and threaded M6/M8 at the hot end. Buy it, don’t turn it. About USD 8.
  • Bearings: two 8×16×5 mm (688-type) angular-contact or hybrid ceramic bearings. The cheapest sensible fix. About USD 15 each.
  • Tunnel: a length of Ø28 steel bar bored out to Ø16 (a lathe job) or a welded Ø28 tube with two bored end blocks. Bolt flange at the front, plate at the rear.
  • Lubrication: a drip of turbine oil or ATF every few runs is the minimum; add 3–5 % two-stroke oil to a liquid fuel; or fit a tiny pump (chapter 8).

Layer 2

Suck & squeeze: the impeller

A 56 mm aluminium wheel with seven full and seven short backswept blades. At 115 000 rpm its tips travel at 337 m/s and fling the air outward, doubling its pressure.

What it does

Air enters the front (the inducer) moving axially, is caught by the twisted blades, turned through 90° and thrown outward at the rim (the exducer) with 340 m/s of swirl. All the energy comes from the blades pushing on the air: $\Delta h_0=U_2C_{\theta2}\approx79$ kJ/kg (chapter 4).

Cheap ways to make it
  • Best bargain: a scrap turbocharger compressor wheel (~56 mm exducer: the Garrett T25/GT28-class, from any scrap yard, USD 30–70). Bore it to Ø8 H7 (a lathe, a collet, a steady hand) and balance it (chapter 8). These wheels are designed for 120 000 rpm.
  • Print & cast: print the STL in castable resin / PLA, make a plaster or investment mould, cast A356 aluminium (+1 % shrink compensation).
  • Mill it from forged 2618-T61 (or 2014-T6/7075-T6) bar on a 4/5-axis CNC; the blades are ruled surfaces so a flank-milled version is possible on 3-axis. Not 6061: at 337 m/s the disc stress is 130–250 MPa (chapter 9) and 6061 has little margin.
  • Aluminium wheels are only OK because the air stays cool (93 °C at the exit); keep a ≥ 1.3× speed margin to burst. A scrap turbo wheel is already proven for this duty.
3
Layer 3

Slow it down: the diffuser

The air leaves the wheel at 340 m/s with far too much kinetic energy and too little pressure. A ring of 15 curved vanes slows it to ~150 m/s and turns the difference into pressure.

What it does

This plate is the back wall of the compressor and carries 15 spiral vanes. The channels between the vanes widen slightly, so the air slows (Bernoulli: slower air has higher pressure). The angled vanes also guide the swirling air outward instead of letting it spin around the gap. After the vanes the air turns 90° and flows aft into the combustor.

The design rule

The pressure you can recover is $C_p=1-1/AR^2$ (AR = area ratio), but only if the flow stays attached. Diverge the walls faster than ~7° (total angle) and the boundary layer separates. Our vanes open by 6°: widen the passage as far as it can stand, no further.

Cheap ways to make it
  • Skip the vanes (vaneless diffuser): a flat plate and a wide gap. 5–10 points less efficiency but no precision parts. Many first builds do exactly this.
  • Sheet vanes: 15 strips of 1 mm stainless, bent on a log-spiral jig and TIG/braze-tacked to a 6 mm aluminium plate.
  • Print the STL in resin to check fit, then machine or cast it in aluminium.
4
Layer 4

Gather it: bellmouth & compressor housing

A trumpet-shaped inlet that lets air in without a fight, wrapped around a casing that hugs the impeller tips within 0.4 mm.

What it does

The bellmouth turns the sideways-converging streamlines from all around into a clean axial stream so the inducer sees a uniform flow. Its curved lip (radius ≈ 1/3 of the inlet diameter) avoids a sharp edge where the air would separate and waste pressure. Further in, the housing wall follows the exact curve of the impeller shroud contour, 0.4 mm away from the blade tips; then it forms the front wall of the diffuser and the bend that turns the flow aft.

Two tolerances rule the compressor: the lip radius and the tip clearance. The gap lets high-pressure air leak back over each blade tip, and the loss is roughly proportional to gap ÷ blade height ($0.4/6\approx7\%$ of the work!).

Cheap ways to make it
  • Spin or hand-form a 2 mm aluminium funnel for the bellmouth (a kitchen funnel is the right shape!); bore the shroud on a lathe.
  • 3D-print the lip and bend in a high-temperature resin; compressor air only reaches ~100 °C.
  • Set the clearance with shims: assemble with the housing slightly loose, spin the wheel by hand, tighten until you feel no rub. An abradable (cast PTFE or silicone) shroud liner lets the blades cut their own tiny groove.

Layer 5

Contain it: the combustor casing

A stainless pipe, 100 mm across, that holds the pressurised air around the flame tube. Two ports: fuel in, igniter in.

What it does

The casing is a low-pressure vessel (about 1 bar above atmospheric) that forces the compressor’s air to flow around the flame tube and enter it through its holes, not leak past. At the front it bolts to the compressor housing flange; at the rear a closing plate with a Ø68 hole passes the nozzle-guide ring. Two threaded bosses carry the fuel feed pipe (3 o’clock) and the igniter (12 o’clock).

Because the pressure is small, the wall can be thin: it’s the heat and thermal growth that matter. The outside of the casing stays at 200–300 °C and the 8 flange bolts see a total end-load of only ~340 N.

Cheap ways to make it
  • Exhaust-pipe stock: a Ø100 (4") 304 stainless exhaust tube, 2 mm wall, from a car-parts shop; cut to 105 mm.
  • Flanges: laser- or waterjet-cut 4 mm stainless plate (an online cutting service will do it from a DXF for ~USD 10 each); TIG-weld to the pipe, then skim flat or lap on a surface plate.
  • Ports: weld in M10 and Ø8 pipe bosses; the igniter boss must be perpendicular to the axis.
  • Use a copper-graphite or ceramic-fibre gasket between flanges.

Layer 6 · the star

Bang: the Coke-can flame tube

A ring-shaped can with three rows of holes: the place where fuel meets air, burns, and is then diluted to a temperature the turbine can live with.

What it does

The flame tube is an annular liner: two concentric tubes, a Coke can (Ø66) on the outside and a Ø34.5 tube on the inside, closed at the front by a domed plate. It sits in the casing with a gap on both sides, so compressor air flows around it and enters through the holes. The air is split into three jobs:

  1. Primary zone (~22 % of the air). Air + all the fuel. Locally rich-to-stoichiometric (φ ≈ 1.3), the flame burns at ~2 000 °C. Swirling jets from the first hole row make a recirculating pocket that anchors the flame so the 60 m/s flow can’t blow it out.
  2. Secondary zone (~30 %). Finishes burning the rich products (CO, soot) and starts cooling.
  3. Dilution zone (~47 %). Big holes dump in the rest of the cool air to bring the gas down to the turbine-inlet temperature (~800 °C) and even out hot spots.

The numbers: hole sizing

The total hole area is fixed by continuity and the pressure you are willing to sacrifice: $\dot m=C_d A\sqrt{2\rho\,\Delta P}$. At ΔP = 3.7 % of the compressor outlet pressure (a good compromise between mixing quality and thrust), the 0.15 kg/s needs 1 400 mm², split by zone. Change the sliders and compare with the STL’s holes; then print the 1:1 template and use it to drill your own can.

Why a can survives at all

Aluminium melts near 650 °C and loses most of its strength by 300 °C, yet the flame is 2 000 °C. The can survives (briefly!) because the cooling film from the holes keeps the wall at 500–650 °C, and because the 0.1 mm wall conducts so little heat along itself. Honest warningThe Coke can is a teaching liner. Expect a few minutes of full power at best, hours at idle. The warped, oxidised can is your cue to upgrade to 0.5 mm 310 stainless (same pattern, ~USD 40 laser-cut) before turbine inlet temperatures climb, and you can print this exact STL to check the stainless version’s fit.
Cheap ways to make it
  • Outer liner: a plain 12 oz can (Ø66 × 122). Cut both ends off with a rotary tool, trim the wall to 87 mm, deburr, then tape on the 1:1 template and drill: 2 mm pilot first, then a step drill. Use a second can inside the first if you want a stiffer wall.
  • Inner liner: Ø34.5 steel exhaust tube (0.5 mm), or a ~60 mm tomato-purée can cut and rolled.
  • Dome: a 2 mm annular washer dished over a hardwood mould with a ball-peen hammer; fold the edges and crimp or rivet to the two tubes. Drill 8 × Ø6 lance holes plus the small cooling holes.
  • Joints: aluminium cannot be soldered or welded easily with hobby kit: use stainless rivets, a few self-tapping screws, or crimp.

Layer 7

Feed & light it: fuel ring and igniter

A ring of stainless tube with eight short lances pushes propane gas straight into the primary zone; a glow or spark plug lights it.

What it does

Propane arrives from the regulator through the side pipe into a Ø5 ring, then through 8 lances that pass through holes in the flame-tube dome. Each lance has a tiny exit hole, and the pressure drop across those holes meters the fuel. The igniter pokes through the casing and the can wall into the primary zone, where it lights the mixture. Once burning, the flame holds itself.

The numbers: how small are the holes?

At full power the engine burns 2.9 g/s of propane (10.4 kg/h; a 9 kg cylinder lasts under an hour). A gas flow through a hole at the regulator pressure is choked above a pressure ratio of ~1.8, so the flow depends only on the area and the upstream pressure. That makes metering easy: the hole sizes below.

Gas safety
  • Use a fail-closed solenoid after the regulator. No power → no gas.
  • Leak-test every joint with soapy water, outdoors, before every session. Never use a naked flame to “check”.
  • Cylinder upright (vapour draw), a flame-trap/flashback arrestor in the line, and the cylinder 3+ m behind the engine.
  • Never run an engine in a garage, shed, or under a roof: exhaust is CO + 800 °C air.
Cheap ways to make it
  • Ring: Ø5 × 0.8 stainless brake line (cheap!), bent round a Ø45 former and brazed or silver-soldered to 8 lances of Ø4 tube.
  • Lance holes: drill with a jeweller’s bit (Ø0.7–0.8 mm) in a pin vise: practise on scrap.
  • Igniter: an M10 glow plug (heater-plug from a model-engine shop, about USD 5) driven from a 2 S LiPo; for a spark use a motorbike spark plug on a piezo or a small coil.
  • Regulator & valve: a BBQ-type propane regulator + a 12 V brass solenoid valve + a needle valve for trimming: about USD 40 total.

Layer 8

Blow & drive: guide vanes and turbine

16 fixed vanes aim the hot gas at the rotor; 19 twisted blades on a thin disc catch it and drive the shaft. The hottest, most highly stressed part of the whole engine.

What they do

The nozzle guide vanes (NGV) are a stationary ring of cambered vanes between two thin cones. They squeeze the flow (area drops from 2 800 to 1 450 mm²), accelerating it to ~310 m/s and turning it 58° into the direction of rotation. The turbine wheel is a disc with 19 blades: they turn the flow back toward the axis, and the swirl momentum they remove is the energy that drives the compressor ($\Delta h_0=U(C_{\theta2}-C_{\theta3})\approx 75$–80 kJ/kg, 12 kW at 0.15 kg/s).

Notice the match with the STL: the rotor blade enters at ~15° and leaves at 60° from the axis, exactly the angles in the solver. A turbine blade root sees a centrifugal stress of $\sigma\approx\tfrac12 k\rho\omega^2(r_t^2-r_h^2)\approx$ 250 MPa while at ~750 °C. Stainless steel creeps to rupture in hours under that; only nickel superalloys (Inconel 625/718/713) qualify (chapter 9 shows the numbers).

Cheap ways to make it
  • Wheel from plate: waterjet a 4 mm Inconel 625 or 718 disc, cut 19 blades from 1.5 mm sheet, twist each ~10° in a vice, and TIG- or laser-weld them into slots in the rim (310S is acceptable only for short, low-speed demonstration runs below ~70 000 rpm). Balance the finished wheel before it ever sees rpm.
  • Turbo turbine: a scrap-turbo turbine wheel (Inconel, radial inflow) is a robust alternative: design a radial-inflow variant and let the junkyard supply an off-the-shelf rotor.
  • NGV ring: 1.5 mm 310 sheet formed into the two cones, 16 vanes cut from the same sheet, curved on a mandrel, spot-welded in slotted holes.
  • Safety margin: the real danger is a bursting disc. Never run a home-made wheel without a containment ring and distance (see Safety).

Layer 9

Push: turbine casing, nozzle & tail cone

A tight shroud around the turbine blades and a gently narrowing nozzle that converts the last of the pressure into a 376 m/s jet.

What they do

The turbine casing holds the NGV ring and then wraps the rotor with a 0.2–0.4 mm tip gap (hot gas leaks over blade tips just as compressor air does). Behind it, the nozzle narrows from Ø60 to a 1 119 mm² ring-shaped exit around a tail cone, supported on three thin struts. The cone hides the shaft nut and gives the flow a smooth, drag-free end.

The nozzle area is the tuning knob: smaller = hotter turbine inlet, higher pressure, higher exit speed, less air. Larger = cool and gentle. You can alter it after the engine is built by fitting a ring or trimming the tip. The widget shows what happens to thrust and temperature at constant rpm: notice how fast the temperature climbs as you close the nozzle.

Cheap ways to make it
  • Nozzle cone: a car-exhaust reducer (cone) from a parts shop, or roll 1.5 mm 304 into a cone and TIG it. Make it oversize first, then reduce.
  • Tail cone: spun, or two cheap steel funnels welded base-to-base; three 1.5 mm strips as struts.
  • Turbine shroud: a rolled and welded ring bored (or ground in place with the wheel, protected, spinning slowly) to leave 0.3 mm: the machinist’s favourite trick.
  • Thermal growth: hot parts grow ~1 % per 700 °C of temperature rise (steel 17 µm/m/K): allow a floating joint, never a rigid one, between hot and cold parts.

Layer 10

Hold it down: stand, starter & plumbing

A rigid test stand, a starter motor, a fuel line and four gauges turn a pile of parts into something you can (carefully) run.

Everything together

The completed engine is now on the left. Switch on “Airflow” and “Heat” in the viewer: the engine is running at partial power right now. Cold air (blue) is sucked in, squeezed, heated around the can (white), burned in the primary zone (orange flame), diluted, accelerated by the vanes, spent in the turbine, and blown out the back. The metal glows according to its real temperature from the model; “thermal cam” shows it in false colour. Then open the simulator to start it yourself.
