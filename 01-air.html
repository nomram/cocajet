Air, gases & chemistry

~25 min5 widgetsno maths beyond a calculator

Before we build anything we need to know what we are pushing around. A jet engine does not “burn fuel to make thrust”. It throws air backwards, and the fuel is only there to make that air throw faster.

Safety: reading is safe. Building and running a gas turbine is not. See Safety first before you touch a tool.

Why start with air?

Look at the engine’s job from the outside. Air goes in the front at ~100 m/s and out of the back at ~380 m/s. That speed-up is the whole product. Everything inside (the wheel that squeezes, the can that burns, the turbine that drives the wheel) exists to give that air extra energy on its way through.

So the engine’s performance is set by three properties of air:

  • How much of it arrives each second (mass flow, kg/s).
  • How hot and how squeezed it is (temperature and pressure: its stored energy).
  • How much of it can burn (the oxygen content, which fixes how much fuel we can add).

What air is made of

Air is a mixture of gases, not a single substance. Click any gas below. Two numbers dominate: nitrogen (about four-fifths) and oxygen (about one-fifth). Everything else adds up to one percent.

Because it is a mixture we use an average molecular mass. Multiply each gas’s share by its mass and add:

$$\bar M = \sum_i x_i M_i \approx 0.7808(28.01)+0.2095(32.00)+0.0093(39.95)+\dots \approx 28.96\ \text{g/mol}$$

That single number gives us the specific gas constant for air, $R = R_u/\bar M = 8.314/0.02896 \approx 287\ \mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$. You will meet 287 in almost every formula in this guide.

The one fact to rememberOnly the 21 % that is oxygen can burn. In our engine three quarters of the oxygen is never used: the spare air is what keeps the metal from melting. A jet engine mostly heats and accelerates nitrogen.

A gas is a fluid (that you can squash)

“Fluid” means anything that flows and takes the shape of its container: liquids and gases. So the maths of water pipes and of air ducts are cousins. The big difference is compressibility: water barely changes volume under pressure, a gas changes a lot.

Notice what happens at engine speeds: at Mach 0.8 the density is already 20 % below its “stagnation” value. If we pretended air were water our predictions would be badly wrong. This is why gas turbines need gas dynamics.

Where pressure and temperature come from

A gas is an enormous crowd of molecules flying in straight lines and bouncing off each other and the walls. That picture explains everything:

  • Pressure is the molecules hammering on the walls: the total push of all the tiny impacts, per unit area.
  • Temperature is how energetic the molecules are. Faster molecules means hotter gas.
  • Density is how many molecules are in a given volume.

Use the sliders: heat the box, add molecules, or shove the piston in. Watch the measured pressure follow the ideal-gas prediction $P\propto NT/V$, and watch the speed histogram match the theoretical Maxwell–Boltzmann curve.

Try this: compression heatsPress “Slam the piston in”. The piston pushes the molecules like a bat pushes a ball, so every collision sends them away faster. The temperature climbs. This is exactly why a bicycle pump gets hot, and why our compressor wheel raises the air temperature by ~90 °C at pressure ratio 2.

The atmosphere: thin air, cold air

The air at ground level is squashed by the weight of all the air above it. Climb and there is less above you, so pressure and density fall: roughly halving every 5.5 km. The temperature also falls (about 6.5 °C per km) up to 11 km. A reference model called the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) lets engineers compare engines fairly.

Two effects fight each other: thin air means less mass flow (less thrust) but cold air is denser and easier to compress (a bit more thrust per kg). Net result at altitude: thrust falls roughly in proportion to density. On a hot summer day at an airfield 1 500 m above sea level a hobby jet can lose a quarter of its thrust. Slide ΔT and altitude to see it.

Why fuel burns: the chemistry

A fuel such as propane is a chain of carbon atoms with hydrogen attached. Burning it means rearranging atoms into more tightly bound molecules (CO₂ and H₂O) and releasing the difference in binding energy as heat. The balanced reaction for propane in air is:

$$\mathrm{C_3H_8} + 5\,\mathrm{O_2} + 18.8\,\mathrm{N_2} \longrightarrow 3\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 4\,\mathrm{H_2O} + 18.8\,\mathrm{N_2} + \text{heat}$$

(The 18.8 N₂ appears on both sides: nitrogen is a spectator, but it has to be heated, which uses up some of the energy. 18.8 = 5 × 3.76, the ratio of nitrogen to oxygen in air.)

Maths: how much air does a kilogram of propane need?

One mole of propane has mass $3(12.011)+8(1.008)=44.1$ g. It needs 5 moles of O₂ (160 g) and, with the accompanying nitrogen, a total of $5\,(31.998 + 3.76\times 28.013)=687$ g of air.

$$\text{AFR}_{\text{stoich}}=\frac{687\ \text{g air}}{44.1\ \text{g fuel}}\approx 15.6\ \text{kg air per kg propane}$$

The equivalence ratio $\varphi=\text{AFR}_{\text{stoich}}/\text{AFR}_{\text{actual}}$ says how rich the mixture is. $\varphi=1$ means perfect proportions; $\varphi<1$ is lean (spare air).

Heat released: the lower heating value of propane is 46.35 MJ/kg. At full power our engine burns 2.9 g/s, so the fuel power is $\dot m_f\,\text{LHV}=0.0029\times 46.35\times10^6\approx 134\ \text{kW}$, the heat output of about 60 kettles.

The puzzle every engine designer faces

A perfectly mixed flame at φ = 1 reaches about 2 000 °C. Nothing we can afford survives that. Yet the turbine can only take roughly 800 °C. The solution: burn at φ ≈ 1 in a small primary zone where the flame is stable, then dilute the hot gas with the leftover air so that, overall, we run at φ ≈ 0.3 (the dotted “whole engine” line). We design this in chapter 6, step 6: it is the entire reason the Coke can is full of holes.

A kilogram of propane carries as much energy as ~50 kg of Li-ion cells. That is why aircraft burn fuel, and why a 134 kW engine of 2.4 kg is possible at all.

What you now know

  • Air is ~78 % nitrogen, ~21 % oxygen. Only the oxygen burns; the rest is working fluid and coolant.
  • A gas is a compressible fluid. $P=\rho R T$ ties its state together; $a=\sqrt{\gamma R T}$ is the speed of pressure waves.
  • Pressure = molecular impacts; temperature = molecular energy; squeezing a gas makes it hotter.
  • Air thins with altitude, so thrust falls with altitude; hot days hurt too.
  • Propane needs about 15.6 kg of air per kg. We run far leaner (≈ 50 kg/kg) so the flame doesn’t melt the engine.
