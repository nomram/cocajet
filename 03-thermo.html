Pressure, heat & the Brayton cycle

~35 min3 big widgetsthe most important chapter in the guide

What happens to a gas when you squeeze it, heat it, and let it expand? Answer that and you understand every jet engine ever built. The answer is a nearly rectangular loop on a graph.

The gas laws: three observations, one equation

Seventeenth-century experimenters squeezed and heated trapped air and found three simple rules:

  • Boyle (1662): at constant temperature, squeeze the volume to half and the pressure doubles: $P\,V=\text{const}$.
  • Charles (1787): at constant pressure, heat it and the volume grows in proportion to the (absolute) temperature: $V/T=\text{const}$.
  • Gay-Lussac (1809): at constant volume, heating raises pressure in proportion to temperature: $P/T=\text{const}$.

Glue them together and you get the ideal gas law. Per kilogram of gas it reads:

$$P\,v = R\,T\qquad\text{or}\qquad P=\rho R T,\qquad R_{\text{air}}=287\ \text{J/(kg K)}$$

where $v=1/\rho$ is the specific volume (m³ per kg). The molecules-in-a-box picture of chapter 1 explains why: pressure is molecular impacts per area ($\propto$ number density × energy), temperature is molecular energy. Absolute temperature (kelvin = °C + 273.15) matters: zero kelvin is where the molecules stop.

Derivation from the molecules-in-a-box picture

A molecule of mass $m$ bouncing between two walls $L$ apart at speed $u_x$ hits a wall $u_x/2L$ times per second and transfers $2mu_x$ each time. A box holds $N$ molecules, and on average a third of the energy is in each direction, $\overline{u_x^2}=\tfrac13\overline{u^2}$. Force on a wall is therefore $\tfrac{N m \overline{u^2}}{3L}$ and pressure (force / $L^2$) is

$$P=\frac{N m\,\overline{u^2}}{3V}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{N}{V}\cdot\tfrac12 m\overline{u^2}$$

Temperature is defined through the average kinetic energy: $\tfrac12 m\overline{u^2}=\tfrac32 k_BT$. Substitute: $PV=Nk_BT$, which is the gas law ($Nk_B=mR$ for a mass $m$ of gas).

Heat, work and the first law

Energy is conserved. A gas can gain energy as heat $Q$ (from a flame) or as work $W$ (from a piston or compressor), and stores it as internal energy $U$, the molecular motion:

$$\Delta U = Q + W_{\text{on gas}},\qquad \Delta U = c_v\,\Delta T,\qquad \text{heat at constant pressure: } Q = c_p\,\Delta T$$

For air, $c_v\approx718$ and $c_p\approx1005\ \mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$, with $c_p-c_v=R$ and a ratio $\gamma=c_p/c_v=1.4$. Hot combustion gas has a bit more “wiggle room” in its molecules (CO₂ and water have more ways to store energy), so $c_p\approx1150$ and $\gamma\approx1.33$ at 1000 K. We use 1.4 for cold air and 1.33 for the hot part.

Squeeze it fast and it gets hot

A compressor wheel passes air through in a few microseconds: no time to lose heat to the casing. That is an adiabatic process ($Q=0$), and all the work goes into temperature:

$$P\,v^{\gamma}=\text{const}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{T_2}{T_1}=\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}=r^{0.286}$$

Take our engine, $r=1.95$, $T_1=288$ K: $T_2=288\times1.95^{0.286}=348$ K, +60 K if ideal. A real wheel is only ~78 % efficient (some work churns the air into extra heat) so the true outlet is 365 K. The work per kg is $c_p\Delta T=1005\times 78\approx 78$ kJ/kg and at 0.151 kg/s that is 12 kW of shaft power, as much as 6 electric kettles. The turbine has to deliver all of it.

The Brayton cycle: the curved rectangle

Now chain four processes together and make a loop. A parcel of air, followed through the engine:

  1. 1 → 2 · Squeeze. Adiabatic compression: pressure and temperature rise, volume shrinks (the curved left edge).
  2. 2 → 3 · Bang. Burn fuel at (nearly) constant pressure: temperature and volume grow (the flat top edge).
  3. 3 → 4 · Blow. Adiabatic expansion through the turbine and nozzle: pressure falls back to ambient, temperature drops (the curved right edge).
  4. 4 → 1 · Cool. The exhaust mixes into the atmosphere at constant pressure and cools (the flat bottom, outside the engine).

On a pressure–volume graph that is a rectangle with two curved sides: the Brayton cycle. The area enclosed is the net work you get from each kilogram of air. On the temperature–entropy graph it appears as a different but equivalent loop. Experiment: the shaded areas are the same energy.

Check this against Chapter 2Press “Our engine (CJ-1)”. The net work is 71 kJ/kg. All of it becomes jet kinetic energy: $\tfrac12V_e^2 = 71\,000\ \text{J/kg}\Rightarrow V_e=\sqrt{2\times71000}\approx377$ m/s. That is the same exhaust speed we used for the thrust (58 N). Thermodynamics and Newton agree, because they are the same energy.

What the loop is telling you

1 · More heat → more work (turn up T₃)

Raise $T_3$ (the turbine inlet temperature) and the loop gets taller: more net work from the same air. This is why metallurgy decides engine power: the hotter the metal can run, the more thrust per kilogram of air. Our can liner limits us to ~1 100 K; a modern airliner with cooled single-crystal blades runs 1 900 K.

2 · More squeeze → better efficiency (turn up r)

The ideal efficiency depends only on the pressure ratio:

$$\eta_{\text{th}}=1-\frac{1}{r^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}}=1-\frac{T_1}{T_2}$$

At $r=2$ that is 18 %; at $r=10$, 48 %; at $r=40$, 65 %. Real values are lower because compressor and turbine aren’t perfect, and the bottom-right panel shows the nasty truth: the lower the component efficiency, the worse it is to raise r, until at some point there is no net work left at all.

3 · There is a best r for work (it is not the highest)

Net work per kg rises with $r$ then falls: very high $r$ means the compressor eats almost everything the turbine makes. For an ideal cycle the maximum-work pressure ratio is $r_{\text{opt}}=(T_3/T_1)^{\gamma/(2(\gamma-1))}$. For $T_3=1070$ K that is r ≈ 10, much higher than ours; with realistic component efficiencies the peak shifts down to r ≈ 4 (look at the dashed curve). We stay at 2 because a bigger wheel or a second stage costs far more than the extra thrust is worth to a hobbyist.

4 · Half the expansion drives the compressor, half makes the jet

In the widget the gas expands all the way back to ambient pressure. In a real jet engine that expansion is split in two: the turbine takes only as much as the compressor needs (here 78 kJ/kg, close to the compressor’s own 78 kJ/kg), and the remaining pressure drop is spent in the nozzle, turning into jet speed (71 kJ/kg net → 377 m/s). In a power turbine (a ship, a power station) the turbine takes everything and delivers shaft power instead.

The sobering consequence: the turbine must deliver almost as much power as it makes to the compressor. Because $W_c/W_t\approx 97\%$ at the real turbine, a few percent of lost efficiency in either wheel eats a big slice of the thrust: that is why chapter 8 spends so long on clearances, balance and blade shape.

Why a Brayton cycle, and not a car-engine cycle?A piston engine runs the loop in four separate strokes inside one cylinder (the Otto cycle). A gas turbine runs all four steps at once, each in its own part of the engine, with the gas flowing continuously through. That makes it light and smooth, and it is why a jet is the best power-to-weight engine we have.

What you now know

  • $Pv=RT$: pressure, volume and temperature of a gas are tied together.
  • Squeezing air fast (adiabatically) heats it: $T_2/T_1=r^{0.286}$. Our wheel adds ~75 K for r ≈ 2 at 78 % efficiency.
  • A jet engine runs the Brayton cycle: squeeze, burn at constant pressure, expand. The enclosed P–V area is net work per kg, and here it all becomes jet speed.
  • Higher $T_3$ → more work. Higher $r$ → better efficiency (but best work at moderate r).
  • The turbine delivers only what the compressor needs (~97 % of its output goes straight back to the compressor); the rest of the expansion happens in the nozzle. Component efficiency matters hugely.
