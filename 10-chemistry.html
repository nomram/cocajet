Chapter 10 · Fire and ignition

The chemistry of heat

~50 min8 widgetsfrom a hand-warmer to a rocket

Every newton of thrust in this guide ultimately comes from one thing: atoms swapping partners and the energy difference leaving as heat. This chapter starts from the very beginning (why does anything get hot? why does a cold pack get cold?) and builds up to the problem every engine designer lives with: a reaction that releases heat speeds itself up, so how do you stop it running away?

Heat is atoms changing partners

Atoms in a molecule are held together by chemical bonds, and every bond stores energy, like a compressed spring. To break a bond you must put energy in. When atoms form a new bond, energy comes out. A chemical reaction is nothing but breaking some bonds and making others, so its heat is the difference:

$$\Delta H \;=\; \underbrace{\sum E_{\text{bonds broken}}}_{\text{energy you pay}} \;-\; \underbrace{\sum E_{\text{bonds made}}}_{\text{energy you get back}}$$
  • If the new bonds are stronger overall, more energy comes out than went in: $\Delta H<0$, the reaction is exothermic (it gets hot). Burning, rusting, neutralising an acid.
  • If the old bonds were stronger, the reaction takes heat from its surroundings: $\Delta H>0$, endothermic (it gets cold, or needs a heater). Melting, evaporating, splitting water with electricity, a cold pack.

Try it: pick a reaction and count the bonds. Notice that burning always turns weak bonds (C–H, O=O) into very strong ones (C=O and O–H in CO₂ and water): that is where the fire comes from.

A rule worth rememberingBurning a hydrocarbon releases very nearly 400 kJ for every mole of oxygen it consumes, whatever the hydrocarbon (methane −401, propane −409, octane −405 kJ per mole O₂). It means oxygen, not fuel, is what sets how much heat you get. That is why a rocket, which has to carry its own oxygen, is so much heavier than a jet engine, which takes it from the air for free.

Enthalpy, and a table of examples

The heat of a reaction at constant pressure is called its enthalpy change $\Delta H$ (kJ per mole of the reaction as written). It can also be built from tabulated heats of formation $\Delta H_f$ of each substance (Hess’s law: the answer doesn’t depend on the route, only on start and end):

$$\Delta H_{\text{reaction}}=\sum \Delta H_f(\text{products})-\sum \Delta H_f(\text{reactants})$$

Worked example, our fuel. $\mathrm{C_3H_8}+5\,\mathrm{O_2}\to3\,\mathrm{CO_2}+4\,\mathrm{H_2O(g)}$ with $\Delta H_f$ = −103.8 (propane), 0 (O₂), −393.5 (CO₂), −241.8 (H₂O vapour) kJ/mol:

$$\Delta H=3(-393.5)+4(-241.8)-(-103.8)=-2043.9\ \text{kJ per mol}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{2043.9\ \text{kJ}}{0.044097\ \text{kg}}=46.35\ \text{MJ/kg}$$

That is the 46.35 MJ/kg the engine model uses as the “lower heating value” of propane.

How much energy per kilogram? (and why the oxygen matters)

Because oxygen sets the heat, the fair way to compare energy sources is per kilogram of everything you have to carry. Flip the switch in the chart: with the oxygen counted, petrol drops from 44 MJ/kg to about 10 MJ/kg, and a surprising fact appears: every conventional explosive carries less energy per kilogram than petrol. What makes them different is not how much energy they hold but how fast they let it go. Rate is the dangerous part, and controlling the rate is what the rest of this chapter is about.

Why doesn’t everything burn at once? Activation energy

A tank of petrol and the air around it sit together all day. The reaction is hugely exothermic and still nothing happens. Why? Before bonds can re-form, the old ones have to be stretched and partly broken: the molecules must climb an energy hill, the activation energy $E_a$. Only the few molecules that collide hard enough at the right moment make it over. At temperature $T$ the fraction with enough energy is the Boltzmann factor:

$$k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}$$

This one formula explains a lot: why a match is needed (it makes a tiny region hot enough for the factor to become large), why every 10 K roughly doubles a reaction rate near room temperature, why food keeps in a fridge, and why a flame at 2000 K is a million times quicker than the same mixture at 300 K. A catalyst doesn’t change the heat released ($\Delta H$ is fixed by start and end): it just lowers the hill.

Runaway: when heat feeds the reaction that makes the heat

Now combine the two facts. An exothermic reaction makes heat. Heat raises the temperature. A higher temperature speeds the reaction exponentially, making more heat. That is a positive feedback loop. The only thing that can stop it is heat loss (conduction, convection, radiation, or being carried away by a flow), which grows only linearly with temperature. So there is a race, and the Russian chemist Nikolai Semenov drew it as two curves:

  • if the heat-loss line cuts the heat-made curve, the temperature settles at the first crossing: a stable, controlled reaction;
  • if the made-curve stays above the loss line everywhere (not enough cooling, too much reactant, too high an ambient temperature), the temperature can only go up: thermal runaway, and then explosion, fire or melted hardware.

Real examples of the same race: oily rags piling up in a warm garage, wet hay or grain, a damaged lithium battery, a chemical reactor that loses its cooling water, a rocket motor whose case is cracked. The common cure is always one of five levers:

A flame is a runaway that is held in check

A combustor wants the reaction to run away, up to the flame temperature, and keeps it there. It does this with a trick: the gas flows through. Fresh cold mixture enters, burns, and leaves, carrying the heat out. In a simple model (a well-stirred reactor) the balance gives an S-shaped curve. If the gas lingers (long residence time $\tau$) the mixture burns completely. If the gas rushes through too fast, the reaction doesn’t have time to finish, the temperature falls, the reaction slows further, and the flame blows out: the same runaway, in reverse. In between is a region where both a lit and an unlit state exist: hysteresis. That is why lighting needs a spark but, once lit, the flame survives conditions that would never have ignited by themselves.

In the engine. The same two ideas (finite reaction time and a flammable window) decide how the CJ-1’s combustor behaves. Efficiency is lowest at idle and at altitude, where pressure and flow are low, and the primary zone, which receives about a quarter of the air, must hold its fuel/air ratio between lean and rich blow-out. The simulator’s lean and wet scenarios (chapter 7) are exactly this chart.

Look at where the CJ-1 sits: the primary zone gives the mixture about 0.75 ms at full power and 1.5 ms at idle, roughly twice the blow-out limit at φ ≈ 1.1. That margin is what the swirl and the recirculating gas in the primary zone buy: they keep gas in the flame zone longer than the throughflow alone would. It also explains the lean-blow-out limit of the engine simulator: below φ ≈ 0.4 in the primary zone the curve’s upper branch disappears and the flame goes out. And it explains why we need a spark at all (next chapter).

Controlling the rate by the size of the pieces

Besides temperature, the second great control on a burn rate is surface area. A reaction between a solid or liquid fuel and air happens at the surface. Cut one cubic centimetre of fuel into ever smaller pieces and the surface explodes, the burn time collapses, and the same fuel goes from smouldering to flashing.

Two engineering consequences. In a jet engine, liquid fuel is sprayed into droplets of ~30 µm so that each burns in about a millisecond: the combustor is only a few centimetres long because of this (propane, being a gas, needs no droplets, which is why our engine is so simple). In solid-propellant rockets the burn rate is tuned by the particle size of the ingredients and by a pinch of catalyst, among other things (chapter 13).

Two ways to release the same energy

Every propulsion system in this guide is a deflagration held at a steady pressure inside a vessel with a hole in it. A rocket motor is built to burn, never to detonate; the whole art is making the burn rate respond gently to pressure and giving the gas a way out. That is the topic of chapter 13.

Handling the chemistry sensibly

  • Keep oxidisers and fuels apart. Many “harmless” solids (fertiliser nitrates, chlorates, perchlorates, pool chemicals, peroxide) are strong oxidisers. Mixed with fuel they can ignite from friction, heat or contamination. Store them dry, in their own containers, away from anything that burns.
  • Quantity matters. The heat released scales with the amount; the heat lost scales with the surface. A small pile cools itself; a big pile runs away. That is why laws set limits on how much you may store.
  • Never mix-and-see. Reactions you have not calculated or looked up are exactly the ones where the loss line loses. Use published data, work small, and work outdoors with eye protection.
  • Know the rules. Fuels, oxidisers and especially energetic materials are regulated almost everywhere. Chapters 13 and 14 describe how rockets work; they are not instructions for making propellants, and in this guide you will not find any.

What you now know

  • Heat of reaction = bonds broken − bonds made. Exothermic reactions (ΔH < 0) release heat; endothermic ones (ΔH > 0) absorb it.
  • Burning hydrocarbons gives ~400 kJ per mole of O₂: oxygen, not fuel, sets the heat. Count the oxidiser you carry and all chemical energy sources are within a factor of two.
  • A reaction needs to climb an activation energy; the rate is $A\,e^{-E_a/RT}$ and doubles every ~10 K near room temperature. Catalysts lower the hill, not ΔH.
  • Heat made grows exponentially with T, heat lost only linearly: if the loss line can’t catch the made-curve, you get thermal runaway. Five levers prevent it: limit the reactant, dilute, cool, negative feedback, relief path.
  • A flame is a stabilised runaway: residence time versus burning speed gives ignition, blow-out and hysteresis. The CJ-1’s primary zone is held a few times above its blow-out limit.
  • Surface area sets the burn rate: finer pieces burn faster (t ∝ d²). Burning (deflagration) and detonation are different physics; rockets are built for the first.
