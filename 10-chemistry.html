The chemistry of heat

~90 min12 widgetsfrom atoms to a rocket

Every newton of thrust in this guide ultimately comes from one thing: atoms swapping partners and the energy difference leaving as heat. This chapter starts from the very beginning (what is a chemical formula? what does “balancing” mean? how many kilograms of air does one kilogram of fuel need?), then asks why anything gets hot and why a cold pack gets cold, and builds up to the problem every engine designer lives with: a reaction that releases heat speeds itself up, so how do you stop it running away?

Everything after them (heat of reaction, rates, runaway, flames) uses that language. If you already know it, jump to “Heat is atoms changing partners”.

Atoms join into molecules by sharing electrons (the bonds of the next section). A chemical formula is the shorthand for one molecule:

  • The letters say which atoms. Capital letters start an element: CO is carbon + oxygen but Co is the metal cobalt.
  • The small subscript after a symbol counts that atom. H₂O is 2 hydrogens and 1 oxygen (no number means 1). C₃H₈ is 3 carbons and 8 hydrogens.
  • A bracket multiplies what is inside it: Ca(OH)₂ is 1 Ca, 2 O and 2 H, five atoms in all.
  • A number in front of a formula is not part of the substance: 3 CO₂ means three separate CO₂ molecules (that number is a coefficient, and it is what balancing changes).
  • Some elements travel in pairs: oxygen is O₂, nitrogen N₂, hydrogen H₂. Lone O atoms are very reactive and do not last.

One extra atom can change everything: CO (carbon monoxide) is a poison, CO₂ is the harmless gas you breathe out. H₂O is water, H₂O₂ is bleaching, rocket-thruster peroxide. N₂O is laughing gas and an oxidiser; NO and NO₂ are the brown smog gases. The widget counts the atoms and adds up the mass.

Balancing: atoms are never created or destroyed

A chemical equation is a sentence: these substances (reactants) turn into those (products). Write propane burning in oxygen and you get the skeleton

$$\mathrm{C_3H_8}+\mathrm{O_2}\;\longrightarrow\;\mathrm{CO_2}+\mathrm{H_2O}$$

but it is wrong, because the left side has 3 carbons and the right side 1, and the left has 8 hydrogens and the right 2. A reaction only rearranges atoms (this is the law of conservation of mass, found by Lavoisier in the 1780s): every atom on the left must reappear on the right. We fix the counts by choosing coefficients. Step by step:

StepWhat you doEquation so far
1Start with the most complicated molecule (propane, 1 of them). Carbon: 3 on the left, so we need 3 CO₂.C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3 CO₂ + H₂O
2Hydrogen: 8 on the left, each H₂O has 2, so we need 4 H₂O.C₃H₈ + O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O
3Oxygen last, because O₂ is alone and flexible. On the right: 3×2 + 4×1 = 10 O atoms, so we need 5 O₂.C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O
4Check every atom: C 3 = 3, H 8 = 8, O 10 = 10. Balanced ✓C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O
The five rules of balancing1. Only change coefficients, never subscripts (changing C₃H₈ to C₃H₉ would be a different substance). 2. Start with the most complicated molecule. 3. Balance the elements that appear in one substance on each side first, usually C then H. 4. Leave a free element such as O₂ for last. 5. If you end up with a fraction, multiply the whole equation to clear it; then check every atom again.

Rule 5 matters for most fuels. Butane gives $\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}+6\tfrac12\mathrm{O_2}\to4\mathrm{CO_2}+5\mathrm{H_2O}$, so double it: $2\,\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}+13\,\mathrm{O_2}\to8\,\mathrm{CO_2}+10\,\mathrm{H_2O}$. Ethanol already contains an oxygen, so it needs less from the air: $\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}+3\,\mathrm{O_2}\to2\,\mathrm{CO_2}+3\,\mathrm{H_2O}$. The “average kerosene” of the engine model, $\mathrm{C_{12}H_{23}}$, needs $17\tfrac34$ O₂ per molecule, so the whole-number version has four molecules: $4\,\mathrm{C_{12}H_{23}}+71\,\mathrm{O_2}\to48\,\mathrm{CO_2}+46\,\mathrm{H_2O}$. Now try it yourself: pick a reaction, use − and + until every row shows ✓, then try typing one of your own into the solver.

Counting by the mole: from an equation to kilograms

The equation C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O counts molecules, but in a workshop we weigh kilograms. The bridge is the mole: 6.022 × 10²³ molecules (Avogadro’s number), chosen so that one mole of any substance weighs its molar mass in grams. One mole of water is 18.0 g, of propane 44.1 g, of oxygen gas O₂ 32.0 g. The equation then also reads: 1 mol of propane (44 g) needs 5 mol of oxygen (160 g). The method for any problem has three steps:

$$\text{mass}\xrightarrow{\ \div M\ }\text{moles}\xrightarrow{\ \times\text{ ratio from the equation}\ }\text{moles of the other substance}\xrightarrow{\ \times M\ }\text{mass}$$

Worked example: 1 kg of propane. Moles of propane: $1000/44.097=22.68$ mol. Oxygen needed: $5\times22.68=113.4$ mol $\times31.998\ \text{g/mol}=3.63$ kg. CO₂ made: $3\times22.68\times44.01=2.99$ kg. Water: $4\times22.68\times18.015=1.63$ kg. Mass in $1+3.63=4.63$ kg; mass out $2.99+1.63=4.62$ kg: mass is conserved, as it must be. One kilogram of burnt propane makes three kilograms of CO₂.

From oxygen to air

Air is 20.95 % oxygen by number of molecules (the same as by volume, see below), but oxygen is heavier than nitrogen, so by mass it is a bit more: weigh a mole of air: $0.7808\times28.01+0.2095\times32.00+0.0093\times39.95\approx28.96$ g, of which oxygen contributes $6.70$ g, so $6.70/28.96=23.1$ % oxygen by mass. Therefore burning 1 kg of propane needs $3.63/0.231=15.7$ kg of air: the stoichiometric air–fuel ratio. It is where “15.6–15.7 kg of air per kg of propane” in chapter 1 comes from.

Our engine passes 0.151 kg of air for every 2.86 g of propane: that is $\varphi=(2.86/151)\big/(1/15.7)=0.30$. The equivalence ratio $\varphi$ is the fuel actually added divided by the fuel that would use up all the oxygen. The engine therefore uses only about 30 % of its oxygen; the other 70 % of the air cools the flame (chapter 6) and leaves unused.

Try it: how much air for the CJ-1?

The engine burns 2.86 g/s of propane. Oxygen needed for a perfect burn: $2.86\times3.63=10.4$ g/s; as air, $10.4/0.231=45$ g/s. The engine actually passes 151 g/s, so $45/151=0.30$: it uses 30 % of the air’s oxygen. CO₂ made: $2.86\times2.99=8.6$ g/s, about half a kilogram a minute.

Gases by the mole: why volume and pressure change

For gases there is a lovely shortcut: equal volumes of any gas at the same pressure and temperature contain the same number of molecules (Avogadro). One mole of any gas occupies about 22.4 litres at 0 °C and 1 atm, and 23.6 L at 15 °C (the volume grows in proportion to the absolute temperature). So the coefficients of a gas reaction are also volume ratios: 1 litre of propane gas needs 5 litres of oxygen and makes 3 litres of CO₂ and 4 of steam.

  • The number of gas molecules changes. Propane + 5 O₂ (6 molecules) becomes 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O (7 molecules): the count rises by 17 %, even before the heat. Where the products are fewer (hydrogen + oxygen: 3 → 2) the mixture shrinks.
  • Heat multiplies it. The gas leaves the flame at 2 000+ K, about seven times its starting absolute temperature, so at constant pressure its volume grows sevenfold more. Squeezed into a closed rocket chamber, that is the pressure of chapter 13.
  • Water as steam or liquid matters. Burning propane to steam releases 2 044 kJ/mol (the “lower heating value”, 46.35 MJ/kg, the figure engines use), but if the steam condenses to liquid, another 4 × 44 kJ = 176 kJ is released, giving the “higher heating value” of 2 220 kJ/mol. A jet engine’s exhaust never condenses inside the engine, so it uses the lower value.

Combine with the ideal gas law $PV=nRT$ from chapter 1 and you can go from kilograms to cubic metres at any temperature and pressure: that is what the engine model does at every station.

What “oxidise” means (and what an oxidiser is)

Burning is one case of a bigger family: oxidation. The old definition is “combining with oxygen”: the fuel is oxidised, the oxygen is reduced (it ends up in CO₂ and H₂O). The modern definition is about electrons, summed up by OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss of electrons, Reduction Is Gain. The two always go together, which is why chemists say redox: something has to take the electrons that something else gave up.

ExampleEquationWhat is oxidisedSpeed

The same overall chemistry can run in milliseconds or in decades, with the same heat released in total: the energy of a reaction and its speed are different questions. The first is the subject of the next sections (ΔH); the second is the Arrhenius law and the runaway problem that follow it.

An oxidiser is any substance that readily supplies the oxygen (or takes the electrons) that a fuel needs. Air is the free one. Rockets carry their own: liquid oxygen, nitrous oxide (which releases oxygen as it decomposes: $2\,\mathrm{N_2O}\to2\,\mathrm{N_2}+\mathrm{O_2}$), hydrogen peroxide, and oxygen-rich salts such as nitrates, chlorates and perchlorates that hold the oxygen inside a solid. Those salts are why a “harmless” white fertiliser or pool chemical needs respect, and why the care section below insists on keeping oxidisers and fuels apart.

Heat is atoms changing partners

With formulas and balanced equations in hand we can ask the first real question: where does the heat come from? Atoms in a molecule are held together by chemical bonds, and every bond stores energy, like a compressed spring. To break a bond you must put energy in. When atoms form a new bond, energy comes out. A chemical reaction is nothing but breaking some bonds and making others, so its heat is the difference:

$$\Delta H \;=\; \underbrace{\sum E_{\text{bonds broken}}}_{\text{energy you pay}} \;-\; \underbrace{\sum E_{\text{bonds made}}}_{\text{energy you get back}}$$
  • If the new bonds are stronger overall, more energy comes out than went in: $\Delta H<0$, the reaction is exothermic (it gets hot). Burning, rusting, neutralising an acid.
  • If the old bonds were stronger, the reaction takes heat from its surroundings: $\Delta H>0$, endothermic (it gets cold, or needs a heater). Melting, evaporating, splitting water with electricity, a cold pack.

Try it: pick a reaction and count the bonds. Notice that burning always turns weak bonds (C–H, O=O) into very strong ones (C=O and O–H in CO₂ and water): that is where the fire comes from.

A rule worth rememberingBurning a hydrocarbon releases very nearly 400 kJ for every mole of oxygen it consumes, whatever the hydrocarbon (methane −401, propane −409, octane −405 kJ per mole O₂). It means oxygen, not fuel, is what sets how much heat you get. That is why a rocket, which has to carry its own oxygen, is so much heavier than a jet engine, which takes it from the air for free.

Enthalpy, and a table of examples

The heat of a reaction at constant pressure is called its enthalpy change $\Delta H$ (kJ per mole of the reaction as written). It can also be built from tabulated heats of formation $\Delta H_f$ of each substance (Hess’s law: the answer doesn’t depend on the route, only on start and end):

$$\Delta H_{\text{reaction}}=\sum \Delta H_f(\text{products})-\sum \Delta H_f(\text{reactants})$$

Worked example, our fuel. $\mathrm{C_3H_8}+5\,\mathrm{O_2}\to3\,\mathrm{CO_2}+4\,\mathrm{H_2O(g)}$ with $\Delta H_f$ = −103.8 (propane), 0 (O₂), −393.5 (CO₂), −241.8 (H₂O vapour) kJ/mol:

$$\Delta H=3(-393.5)+4(-241.8)-(-103.8)=-2043.9\ \text{kJ per mol}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{2043.9\ \text{kJ}}{0.044097\ \text{kg}}=46.35\ \text{MJ/kg}$$

That is the 46.35 MJ/kg the engine model uses as the “lower heating value” of propane.

Balancing and ΔH go together$\Delta H$ belongs to the equation as written, so it scales with the coefficients and changes sign when you reverse the reaction. Hydrogen burning: $2\,\mathrm{H_2}+\mathrm{O_2}\to2\,\mathrm{H_2O}$ gives $\Delta H=-483.7$ kJ; written as $\mathrm{H_2}+\tfrac12\mathrm{O_2}\to\mathrm{H_2O}$ it is $-241.8$ kJ (the same reaction per mole of water; half-molecules are fine in thermochemistry because a coefficient counts moles); and the reverse, splitting water, $2\,\mathrm{H_2O}\to2\,\mathrm{H_2}+\mathrm{O_2}$, is $+483.7$ kJ. How much energy per kilogram? (and why the oxygen matters)

Because oxygen sets the heat, the fair way to compare energy sources is per kilogram of everything you have to carry. Flip the switch in the chart: with the oxygen counted, petrol drops from 44 MJ/kg to about 10 MJ/kg, and a surprising fact appears: every conventional explosive carries less energy per kilogram than petrol. What makes them different is not how much energy they hold but how fast they let it go. Rate is the dangerous part, and controlling the rate is what the rest of this chapter is about.

How hot does the flame get?

Now use the balanced equation to predict a temperature. The heat released has to go somewhere: into warming the products and the nitrogen that came along. For one kilogram of propane burnt exactly: 46.35 MJ of heat is shared by 1 kg of fuel plus 15.7 kg of air, almost 16.7 kg of gas whose average specific heat is about 1.3 kJ/(kg·K):

$$\Delta T\approx\frac{Q}{m\,c_p}=\frac{46\,350\ \text{kJ}}{16.7\ \text{kg}\times1.3\ \text{kJ/(kg K)}}\approx2\,100\ \text{K}\ \Rightarrow\ T_{flame}\approx2\,400\ \text{K}\ (2\,100\ ^\circ\text{C})$$

This is the adiabatic flame temperature (no heat lost). The same bookkeeping explains three facts at once. First, a lean flame is cooler because the same heat warms more air (at φ = 0.5 the gas reaches only ~1 500 K). Second, a rich flame is cooler too, because the missing oxygen means not all the heat is released. Third, the highest temperature sits near φ = 1, and it is not as high as the simple sum predicts, because of dissociation.

Chemical reactions can run both ways. At everyday temperatures CO₂ is the stable end of the road, but a flame at 2 300 K is hot enough that some CO₂ splits back: $\mathrm{CO_2}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{CO}+\tfrac12\mathrm{O_2}$, and some water too: $\mathrm{H_2O}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{H_2}+\tfrac12\mathrm{O_2}$. That splitting is endothermic, so it soaks up heat and holds the flame about 100 K cooler. How far it goes is set by an equilibrium constant $K(T)$ that grows steeply with temperature (for the CO₂ split it is 0.0013 at 2 000 K and 0.036 at 2 500 K, 28 times larger) and is suppressed by pressure, as Le Chatelier’s principle says: a system squeezed from outside shifts toward the side with fewer molecules. The widget solves both the energy balance and these two equilibria.

Why doesn’t everything burn at once? Activation energy

A tank of petrol and the air around it sit together all day. The reaction is hugely exothermic and still nothing happens. Why? Before bonds can re-form, the old ones have to be stretched and partly broken: the molecules must climb an energy hill, the activation energy $E_a$. Only the few molecules that collide hard enough at the right moment make it over. At temperature $T$ the fraction with enough energy is the Boltzmann factor:

$$k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}$$

This one formula explains a lot: why a match is needed (it makes a tiny region hot enough for the factor to become large), why every 10 K roughly doubles a reaction rate near room temperature, why food keeps in a fridge, and why a flame at 2000 K is a million times quicker than the same mixture at 300 K. A catalyst doesn’t change the heat released ($\Delta H$ is fixed by start and end): it just lowers the hill.

Runaway: when heat feeds the reaction that makes the heat

Now combine the two facts. An exothermic reaction makes heat. Heat raises the temperature. A higher temperature speeds the reaction exponentially, making more heat. That is a positive feedback loop. The only thing that can stop it is heat loss (conduction, convection, radiation, or being carried away by a flow), which grows only linearly with temperature. So there is a race, and the Russian chemist Nikolai Semenov drew it as two curves:

  • if the heat-loss line cuts the heat-made curve, the temperature settles at the first crossing: a stable, controlled reaction;
  • if the made-curve stays above the loss line everywhere (not enough cooling, too much reactant, too high an ambient temperature), the temperature can only go up: thermal runaway, and then explosion, fire or melted hardware.

Real examples of the same race: oily rags piling up in a warm garage, wet hay or grain, a damaged lithium battery, a chemical reactor that loses its cooling water, a rocket motor whose case is cracked. The common cure is always one of five levers:

A flame is a runaway that is held in check

A combustor wants the reaction to run away, up to the flame temperature, and keeps it there. It does this with a trick: the gas flows through. Fresh cold mixture enters, burns, and leaves, carrying the heat out. In a simple model (a well-stirred reactor) the balance gives an S-shaped curve. If the gas lingers (long residence time $\tau$) the mixture burns completely. If the gas rushes through too fast, the reaction doesn’t have time to finish, the temperature falls, the reaction slows further, and the flame blows out: the same runaway, in reverse. In between is a region where both a lit and an unlit state exist: hysteresis. That is why lighting needs a spark but, once lit, the flame survives conditions that would never have ignited by themselves.

In the engine. The same two ideas (finite reaction time and a flammable window) decide how the CJ-1’s combustor behaves. Efficiency is lowest at idle and at altitude, where pressure and flow are low, and the primary zone, which receives about a quarter of the air, must hold its fuel/air ratio between lean and rich blow-out. The simulator’s lean and wet scenarios (chapter 7) are exactly this chart.

Look at where the CJ-1 sits: the primary zone gives the mixture about 0.75 ms at full power and 1.5 ms at idle, roughly twice the blow-out limit at φ ≈ 1.1. That margin is what the swirl and the recirculating gas in the primary zone buy: they keep gas in the flame zone longer than the throughflow alone would. It also explains the lean-blow-out limit of the engine simulator: below φ ≈ 0.4 in the primary zone the curve’s upper branch disappears and the flame goes out. And it explains why we need a spark at all (next chapter).

Controlling the rate by the size of the pieces

Besides temperature, the second great control on a burn rate is surface area. A reaction between a solid or liquid fuel and air happens at the surface. Cut one cubic centimetre of fuel into ever smaller pieces and the surface explodes, the burn time collapses, and the same fuel goes from smouldering to flashing.

Two engineering consequences. In a jet engine, liquid fuel is sprayed into droplets of ~30 µm so that each burns in about a millisecond: the combustor is only a few centimetres long because of this (propane, being a gas, needs no droplets, which is why our engine is so simple). In solid-propellant rockets the burn rate is tuned by the particle size of the ingredients and by a pinch of catalyst, among other things (chapter 13).

Two ways to release the same energy

A reacting front moves through a material in one of two completely different ways:

Deflagration (a normal flame)Detonation
Front speedmillimetres to a few metres per second, subsonic1.5 to 9 kilometres per second, supersonic
What drives itheat conducted and mixed ahead of the flamea shock wave that compresses and ignites the material
Pressurenearly constant: the gas expands away from the flamea violent spike, 15–30 times the starting pressure

Every propulsion system in this guide is a deflagration held at a steady pressure inside a vessel with a hole in it. A rocket motor is built to burn, never to detonate; the whole art is making the burn rate respond gently to pressure and giving the gas a way out. That is the topic of chapter 13.

Handling the chemistry sensibly

  • Keep oxidisers and fuels apart. Many “harmless” solids (fertiliser nitrates, chlorates, perchlorates, pool chemicals, peroxide) are strong oxidisers. Mixed with fuel they can ignite from friction, heat or contamination. Store them dry, in their own containers, away from anything that burns.
  • Quantity matters. The heat released scales with the amount; the heat lost scales with the surface. A small pile cools itself; a big pile runs away. That is why laws set limits on how much you may store.
  • Never mix-and-see. Reactions you have not calculated or looked up are exactly the ones where the loss line loses. Use published data, work small, and work outdoors with eye protection.
  • Know the rules. Fuels, oxidisers and especially energetic materials are regulated almost everywhere. Chapters 13 and 14 describe how rockets work; they are not instructions for making propellants, and in this guide you will not find any.

What you now know

  • A formula counts atoms (subscripts); a coefficient counts molecules. A mole is 6.022 × 10²³ molecules and weighs the molar mass in grams.
  • Balance an equation by changing coefficients only, one element at a time (C, then H, then O last), and clear fractions by doubling. Atoms, and therefore mass, are conserved.
  • Equation → kilograms: mass ÷ M → moles → coefficient ratio → × M. Propane needs 3.63 kg of oxygen, 15.7 kg of air, and makes 3 kg of CO₂ per kilogram burnt. The engine runs at φ ≈ 0.30.
  • Lean means spare oxygen, rich means spare fuel; the one that runs out first is the limiting reagent. Too little oxygen gives CO, soot and less heat.
  • Oxidation is loss of electrons; an oxidiser supplies the oxygen. The heat of a reaction and its speed are different questions.
  • Heat of reaction = bonds broken − bonds made. Exothermic reactions (ΔH < 0) release heat; endothermic ones (ΔH > 0) absorb it.
  • Burning hydrocarbons gives ~400 kJ per mole of O₂: oxygen, not fuel, sets the heat. Count the oxidiser you carry and all chemical energy sources are within a factor of two.
  • A reaction needs to climb an activation energy; the rate is $A\,e^{-E_a/RT}$ and doubles every ~10 K near room temperature. Catalysts lower the hill, not ΔH.
  • Heat made grows exponentially with T, heat lost only linearly: if the loss line can’t catch the made-curve, you get thermal runaway. Five levers prevent it: limit the reactant, dilute, cool, negative feedback, relief path.
  • A flame is a stabilised runaway: residence time versus burning speed gives ignition, blow-out and hysteresis. The CJ-1’s primary zone is held a few times above its blow-out limit.
  • The flame temperature follows from an energy balance on the balanced equation (about 2 300 K for propane–air), held down by dissociation. The turbine limit forces the combustor to dilute: only about a quarter of the air goes through the flame.
  • Surface area sets the burn rate: finer pieces burn faster (t ∝ d²). Burning (deflagration) and detonation are different physics; rockets are built for the first.
