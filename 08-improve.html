Make it better, cheaply

~45 min4 widgetsevery upgrade priced

Our engine works, but it is a wasteful one: it burns 10 kg of propane an hour to make 58 N. Almost every improvement from here on is a trade between money, machining skill and risk. This chapter ranks them so you spend where it pays.

Before you upgrade anythingEvery item below that raises speed, pressure or temperature also raises the stored energy and the failure rate. Chapter 9 explains the metals, and the safety page explains why “one more step up” is how people get hurt. Upgrade efficiency first, then consider more power.

The loss budget: where does the fuel go?

Chapter 3 told us a perfect version of our cycle would have a thermal efficiency of 1 − 1/r0.286 ≈ 18 % at pressure ratio 2.0. We get about 8 %. The gap is a pile of small imperfections: air that is not diffused cleanly, a turbine that wastes 20 % of the energy it is given, gas that leaks around the blade tips, a flame that does not burn every molecule, and bearings that drag.

The cleanest way to rank them is to ask the engine model (the same one that drives the simulator): fix just this one imperfection, leave everything else, and re-solve the engine at the same speed. How much less fuel does each newton of thrust cost?

Three things stand out:

  • The diffuser and turbine are worth more than the flame. A 12-point better diffuser recovers pressure we already paid for, and the turbine is a bottleneck because every joule it takes from the gas goes straight back into the compressor, so its inefficiency is paid twice.
  • Tip clearance is the best money-for-effect fix there is. It costs nothing but careful machining: 0.15 mm instead of 0.40 mm is 4 % less fuel.
  • No single fix is dramatic. It is the stack that matters. All eight together cut the fuel per newton by 28 %, and run the turbine 150 K cooler at the same speed, a big gain in blade life.

Balance: the cheapest reliability upgrade

A perfectly built rotor still has a centre of mass a few microns off the shaft axis. That offset spins around at 1 900 Hz and shakes the bearings with a force that grows with the square of the speed:

$$F = U\,\omega^2 \qquad\text{with}\qquad U = m\,e\ \text{(g·mm)}$$

One g·mm of unbalance (a 0.1 g blob 10 mm from the axis) is 145 N at 115 000 rpm: 76 times the weight of our 195 g rotor. The ISO 1940 balance grade G is the speed of the centre of mass as it whirls, $G = e\,\omega$ in mm/s, and sets the allowed unbalance. Turbomachinery wants G2.5 or better.

Worked example. Rotor 195 g at 115 000 rpm ($\omega = 12\,043$ rad/s). For G2.5:

$$e=\frac{G}{\omega}=\frac{2.5\ \text{mm/s}}{12\,043\ \text{s}^{-1}}=0.21\ \mu\text{m},\qquad U=m\,e=195\ \text{g}\times 0.00021\ \text{mm}\approx 0.04\ \text{g·mm}$$

So the whole rotor must balance to better than the weight of a grain of sand at 10 mm radius. That is why people with turbo shops do it on a two-plane dynamic balancer, and why you remove material by tiny grinding passes on the compressor nut and the turbine hub, never by drilling or filing on a blade.

  • Cheap route: take the rotor (wheel + shaft + nut) to a turbocharger shop. Balancing a turbo core is routine and costs the price of a pizza.
  • DIY route: balance each wheel on knife-edge rails or a magnetic balancer first, then trim the assembled rotor on its own bearings while watching a vibration sensor.
  • Never run a rotor you have not balanced after its last machining operation.

Bearings and lubrication

The two bearings on our 8 mm shaft run at 115 000 rpm: a surface speed of 48 m/s, and a DN value (bore in mm × rpm) of 0.9 million. Ordinary steel ball bearings, grease-packed and rated for 20 000 rpm, will not live.

Whatever you choose, the bearing must stay cool: that is the job of the bearing tunnel in the STL, of the air gap between it and the flame tube, and of the cooling flow of oil. A bearing that reaches 150–200 °C loses its hardness and fails in a few seconds. Always keep the oil flowing for a minute after shutdown (heat soak).

Part-by-part tricks that cost little

COMPRESSOR

Shape, not size

Backward-swept blades (25–30°) and a splitter blade between each pair (9 + 9) give about 10 % more thrust at the same speed (pressure ratio 1.94 → 2.02). Scrap turbo wheels already have it.

DIFFUSER

Slow the air gently

A vaneless plate recovers ~70 % of the exit velocity head. 12–16 wedge vanes at the right angle recover 80 % or more. Cut them from sheet and braze.

COMBUSTOR

Hole pattern is everything

Three rows of holes (primary, secondary, dilution) in the proportions of chapter 5. A swirl of primary air holds the flame; bend a few tabs to increase it.

TURBINE

Twist the blades

Hub-to-tip twist (chapter 4) matches the local flow angle. A cheap forming jig gives +4 points of efficiency.

NOZZLE

Right-size the throat

A smaller throat gives more thrust at a hotter turbine. Choose it by the engine’s EGT, not by the thrust target.

INLET

A bellmouth is free thrust

A smooth rounded entry avoids a 2–3 % pressure drop. Spin one from sheet or print it in resin.

More on diffusers, and why they are the biggest single lever

The impeller throws air out at about 280 m/s, a lot of kinetic energy that does not (yet) count as pressure. A diffuser that slows it to 70 m/s turns that kinetic energy into pressure, following Bernoulli: $\Delta P \approx \tfrac12\rho(V_2^2 - V_3^2)$. But air resists being decelerated: slow it too fast and the flow separates from the walls and the recovery vanishes. A vaned diffuser has a throat followed by a gently-opening passage with a total opening angle of at most 8–10°: faster than that and the flow separates. In our model, going from 70 % to 82 % recovery cuts fuel by 7 % and also drops the turbine temperature by 41 K at the same speed.

More thrust: the honest options

All thrust upgrades fall into three families, and each pays in a different currency:

  1. More air (bigger compressor, same speed). Costs machining time and a new, larger frame. At the same tip speed the air speeds stay the same and the flow scales with the inlet area, so thrust goes as $D^2$. A 70 mm impeller (1.25× larger) would give about 1.56 × 58 ≈ 90 N, at 92 000 rpm.
  2. More pressure (faster or better-swept impeller). Thrust and efficiency both rise, but stress rises as $N^2$ and the material demands steepen quickly.
  3. More temperature (richer, with a smaller nozzle). Thrust rises, but each 25 K costs ¾ of the turbine blade’s life (creep, chapter 9).
Rule of thumbMake it more efficient before you make it more powerful. A more efficient engine makes the same thrust at a lower temperature and speed: more power and longer life from the same parts.

Afterburner: thrust you can burn

Our exhaust is 650 °C, and it still has a lot of unused oxygen: the engine runs very lean. Spray fuel into the jet pipe and light it, and that oxygen burns to raise the exhaust temperature, say to 1 100 °C, with no turbine in the way to limit it. The nozzle exit velocity goes as $\sqrt{T}$, so thrust rises by $\sqrt{1\,373/943}\approx 1.21$, i.e. +21 %, for about 1.6 g/s of extra propane (+56 % fuel). Military jets do this for brief bursts. It is a fun experiment (a ring of fuel jets and a flameholder tab in the nozzle) but it needs a longer, heat-resistant nozzle tube with a larger throat (hotter gas flows less easily and would otherwise choke the turbine), makes it much louder, and makes a fireball in front of you. We don’t recommend it for a first engine.

Go deeperIf the fan is the efficient part, why not drive a fan with a motor and skip the turbine? Chapter 15 compares the two honestly: the electric fan is 27× more efficient but a battery holds 70× less energy than propane.

Adding a fan: the turbofan idea

Chapter 2 showed that thrust is cheaper when you accelerate lots of air gently. A turbofan does exactly that: leave the core as it is, but bolt a ducted fan in front and drive it with a second turbine behind the first. The bypass ratio B is the cold air through the fan divided by the hot air through the core.

Set the flight speed to 100 m/s (a fast model aircraft) and look at the right-hand plot: the plain jet (B = 0) wastes more than half of its energy in the exhaust, while B = 6 lifts the propulsive efficiency to about 75 %. Airliners go to B = 10–12 for this reason. On a test stand there is no gain in efficiency (nothing moves), and the fan needs a second shaft, a second turbine and extra bearings: the turbofan only pays off for something that flies.

Every hobby turbine manufacturer’s secret is the controller, not the mechanics. A handful of parts turns our simulator’s “ECU” into reality:

A good controller does five things: it follows a start script (starter → ignition → fuel ramp), it limits EGT by trimming fuel, it limits rpm, it detects flame-out (rpm falls with no fuel change) and it kills the fuel if anything is out of range. These are the same rules the simulator in chapter 7 runs: try breaking each of them there first.

A sensible build roadmap

  1. Simulate first. Run every scenario in chapter 7 until the failure modes feel familiar.
  2. Buy a scrap turbo and study it. Spin the wheel by hand and check for play.
  3. Bench-test only the cold parts: spin the rotor with its electric starter to 10–20 % speed and measure the real pressure ratio and vibration. No flame yet.
  4. Build the combustor and test it alone with a leaf-blower and a small fuel flow.
  5. Assemble the engine on a test stand with a remote start and a fuel cut-off, behind a barrier.
  6. Run short, cold-limited tests (30 s, EGT limit low). Log everything. Look for vibration, leaks and glowing parts.
  7. Improve one thing at a time (this chapter’s list) and re-run the same test. If it got worse, you learned something.

What you now know

  • A modest engine wastes most of its fuel in a handful of places; model sensitivity ranks them: diffuser, turbine, flame, clearance, combustor pressure drop.
  • The “cheap and safe set” (bearings, tip clearance, bellmouth, liner, diffuser) is worth +14 % thrust and −17 % fuel per newton for about USD 270, and makes the turbine cooler.
  • Rotating unbalance force goes as $U\omega^2$; balancing to G2.5 means below 0.04 g·mm for our rotor.
  • Making the turbine hotter or faster is free and dangerous; the careful path is efficiency first.
  • A bypass fan raises thrust and efficiency, at the cost of a second turbine and shaft.
  • The controller is the secret weapon: EGT limit, rpm limit, flame detection, fuel cut.
