Rockets: the other way to push

~50 min5 widgetsbuild a water rocket

Everything you have learned so far still applies: thrust is still momentum thrown backwards, and the nozzle is still where the hot gas turns its heat into speed. One thing changes. A jet engine gets its oxygen from the air it flies through; a rocket carries its own. That single difference makes a rocket work in space, makes it independent of speed and altitude, and makes it burn through propellant eight times faster. This chapter shows why, and builds up the one tool every rocket needs: the nozzle.

The same equation, one term fewer

Recall the thrust equation from chapter 2:

$$F=\underbrace{\dot m\,(V_e-V_0)}_{\text{jet: pays for the air it swallows}}+(P_e-P_0)A_e\qquad\text{rocket: }\ F=\dot m_p\,V_e+(P_e-P_0)A_e$$

The jet’s $\dot m$ is mostly air that it picks up from the free stream moving at $V_0$ and must accelerate to $V_e$. The rocket’s $\dot m_p$ is propellant that was already on board and at rest: no incoming momentum, so no $-\dot m V_0$ drag term. Three consequences:

  • Thrust doesn’t depend on speed. A jet’s thrust sags as you fly faster (the $V_0$ is subtracted) and as the air thins (less $\dot m$).
  • It works in a vacuum, where there is no air to push on or to breathe.
  • You pay for the oxygen. A jet’s propane is 2 % of the mass flow; a rocket’s propellant is 100 % of it, and about three-quarters of that mass is oxidiser.

Specific impulse: miles per gallon for rockets

To compare engines of any size and any type, divide the thrust by the weight-rate of propellant used. The result, in seconds, is the specific impulse:

$$I_{sp}=\frac{F}{\dot m_p\,g_0}=\frac{V_{e,\text{effective}}}{g_0}\quad[\text{s}]$$

It is the number of seconds one kilogram of propellant can produce one kilogram-force of thrust. Higher is more efficient. For the CJ-1: $58\ \text{N}\ /\ (0.00286\ \text{kg/s}\times9.81)=$ 2 070 s, because only fuel counts (the air is free). A kerosene–oxygen rocket manages about 330 s.

The rocket equation, and why we use stages

A rocket has to accelerate the propellant it has not yet burned. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky worked out in 1903 what that does to the speed gained:

$$\Delta v=I_{sp}\,g_0\,\ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}$$

$m_0$ is the starting mass, $m_f$ what is left when the propellant is gone. The speed grows only with the logarithm of the mass ratio: to double your $\Delta v$ you must square the mass ratio. Low Earth orbit needs about 9.4 km/s once gravity and drag are paid. With $I_{sp}=330$ s that is a mass ratio of $e^{9400/(330\times9.81)}=18$: 94 % of the rocket must be propellant, and the tanks, engines, and payload share the remaining 6 %. A rocket whose structure alone is 9 % of its propellant mass simply cannot do it in one piece.

Staging fixes this: throw away tanks and engines as soon as they are empty. For $n$ identical stages with structure fraction $\varepsilon$ and an overall payload fraction $\pi$:

$$\lambda=\pi^{1/n},\quad R=\frac{1}{\lambda+\varepsilon(1-\lambda)},\quad \Delta v_{\text{total}}=n\,I_{sp}\,g_0\ln R$$

Worked example. Kerosene–oxygen ($I_{sp}=350$ s), $\varepsilon=0.10$, payload 2 % of liftoff mass. One stage: $R=1/(0.02+0.098)=8.5$, $\Delta v=350\times9.81\times\ln8.5=7.3$ km/s: short of orbit. Two stages: $\lambda=0.141$, $R=1/(0.141+0.1\times0.859)=4.4$, $\Delta v=2\times350\times9.81\times\ln4.4=10.2$ km/s: orbit, with margin. Try it:

The nozzle: the venturi that makes the thrust

Whatever makes the hot gas, the thrust comes from the nozzle, and the idea is as old as the steam turbine. A venturi is a duct that narrows and widens again. In a liquid, narrowing speeds the flow up and drops the pressure (Bernoulli). A carburettor sucks fuel through exactly that effect; a venturi flow-meter measures flow with it. In a gas something extra happens. The throat speed can rise only until it reaches the speed of sound. At that point the flow is choked: lowering the pressure downstream no longer changes the flow through the throat. The throat has become a valve whose opening alone decides the mass flow, and the gas downstream is free to expand without “telling” the chamber.

Carl Gustaf de Laval, building steam turbines in 1888, realised that if the duct widens after the throat, the supersonic gas goes on accelerating: in supersonic flow a larger area means higher speed, the opposite of what we know from water in a hose. That is the converging–diverging (de Laval) nozzle, the shape of every rocket bell. It converts the heat of the chamber into speed:

$$V_e=\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}\,\frac{R_u T_c}{\mathcal M}\left[1-\left(\frac{P_e}{P_c}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right]}\qquad c^*=\frac{\sqrt{R T_c}}{\sqrt{\gamma}\,\big(\tfrac{2}{\gamma+1}\big)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}}\qquad F=C_F\,P_c\,A_t,\quad I_{sp}=\frac{C_F\,c^*}{g_0}$$

Look at what controls the speed: a hot chamber ($T_c$), a light exhaust gas (small molar mass $\mathcal M$), and a big pressure ratio $P_c/P_e$. That is why hydrogen (molar mass 2, exhaust 12) beats kerosene (exhaust 23) by 100 s of Isp at the same flame temperature. Play with the nozzle: the red line of Mach number rises smoothly through 1 at the throat; add area and the exhaust speed rises; fly higher and the same nozzle gains thrust, but a fixed bell is never perfect everywhere.

Why the turbojet has no bellThe CJ-1’s nozzle sees a pressure ratio of only 1.3, below the critical 1.85 (for hot gas), so the flow leaves at Mach 0.63 and is never choked: a plain convergent pipe is already optimal, and a diverging section would just slow it down. A rocket chamber at 7 MPa is 70 times the ambient pressure: choked in the throat, supersonic in the bell, Mach 3 to 5 at the exit.

Over- and under-expansion

The thrust equation has a pressure term $(P_e-P_a)A_e$. A nozzle designed for sea level (exit pressure ≈ 1 atm) is over-expanded at low altitude if its bell is too big (exit pressure below ambient, losing thrust, and if $P_e/P_a<0.4$ the flow separates from the wall with violent side loads), and under-expanded as it climbs (exit pressure above ambient: the bell is too short to extract everything). Real rockets use a compromise bell on the first stage and a huge one (ε of 100–200) on the vacuum stage. Dual-bell and aerospike nozzles change shape or let the exhaust find its own boundary to avoid the problem.

Your first rocket: a water rocket

You can experience all of this with a plastic soda bottle, water and a bicycle pump. It is the safest real rocket: the “propellant” is water pushed out by compressed air. The water leaves at $v=\sqrt{2\Delta P/\rho}$, the thrust is $F=\dot m\,v=2C_dA_n(P-P_a)$, and the pressure falls as the air expands to fill the space the water leaves. Notice the optimum: about a third of the bottle full.

Build and fly it safely

  • Use PET soda bottles only (carbonated-drink bottles are made for 6–8 bar). Never glass, never a bottle with scratches or dents, never old, sun-damaged plastic.
  • Stay at or below ~7 bar (100 psi) gauge and use a pump with a pressure gauge. Never use anything but air or water: no combustible gas, no CO₂ cartridges, no “just a bit more”.
  • Launch from a launcher (a tube or a quick-release coupling) that you can trigger from a distance with a string or a cable. Nobody ever leans over a pressurised bottle.
  • Safe distance: at least 5–10 m from the launcher to people, eye protection on, in an open field, with nothing overhead and no aircraft, power lines or buildings downrange.
  • Wait for the pressure to be released (and for the rocket to be clearly on the ground) before approaching a misfire. A bottle that failed to launch is still a pressure vessel.
  • Fins and a nose cone make it stable: the centre of gravity must be well ahead of the centre of drag. A bottle flies backwards if it is nose-light.

Try the widget with the nozzle diameter: a smaller nozzle burns longer at lower thrust, a larger one burns out almost instantly. That trade-off, thrust against burn time at the same total impulse, is the heart of every rocket design.

What you now know

  • A rocket’s thrust is $\dot m_pV_e+(P_e-P_a)A_e$: no incoming-air term, so it works at any speed or altitude, and in a vacuum. It pays by carrying the oxidiser: 8× more propellant per newton-second than a turbojet.
  • Specific impulse $I_{sp}=F/(\dot m g_0)$ in seconds is the efficiency measure: 3–10 s (water rocket) to 450 s (hydrogen/oxygen) for chemical rockets; 2 000 s for our jet; thousands for electric thrusters.
  • $\Delta v=I_{sp}g_0\ln(m_0/m_f)$: logarithmic, so structure fraction and staging decide whether a rocket reaches orbit.
  • The converging–diverging nozzle chokes at the throat (Mach 1) and accelerates the gas to Mach 3–5 in the bell. Exhaust speed rises with chamber temperature and falls with exhaust molar mass.
  • A fixed nozzle is only perfect at one altitude; over- and under-expansion cost thrust.
  • A water rocket is the safe laboratory: thrust = 2CdA(P − Pa), best with about 1/3 water.
