Chapter 15 · Electric propulsion

Electric jets: fans, ions and wind

~50 min4 widgetsthe last piece of the map

Every engine so far got its energy from a chemical reaction in the same stream of gas it threw backwards. An electric engine breaks that link: the energy arrives through a wire, and the working fluid is just something to push. That frees the exhaust speed from the chemistry (an ion thruster throws atoms ten times faster than any flame) and it moves the problem somewhere else: you need a lot of electrical power, and a kilogram of battery stores a small fraction of what a kilogram of fuel does. This chapter works through the three electric “jets” (the ducted fan, the ion thruster and the ion wind) and ends with one picture that places every engine of the book.

The electric jet: a fan in a duct

A ducted fan is the simplest engine there is: a motor spins a fan that accelerates a column of air, and the reaction is thrust $F=\dot m(V_e-V_0)$. There is nothing to ignite and nothing to cool but the motor. Treat the fan as an actuator disk: it adds power $P$ to a stream of air of density $\rho$ leaving through an exit area $A_e$. With no forward speed, $P=\tfrac12\dot mV_e^2$ and $\dot m=\rho A_eV_e$, which gives

$$F=\big(4\rho A_e\big)^{1/3}\,P^{2/3}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{F}{P}=\frac{2}{V_e}\ \ (\text{ideal})$$

Two things follow. First, thrust grows only as the two-thirds power of the power: doubling the power buys 59 % more thrust. Second, the way to get more thrust per watt is a bigger disk and a slower jet: a large slow jet is efficient, a small fast one is wasteful (exactly the propulsive-efficiency lesson of chapter 14, and the reason a helicopter rotor is huge and a jet engine is small). A 90 mm fan fed with 4.5 kW of electricity makes about 55 N, close to our CJ-1, at a jet speed of 90 m/s compared with the CJ-1’s 570 m/s.

Now compare honestly with the propane turbojet. Per kilowatt drawn from its energy source, the fan makes about 12 N per kW of electricity, and the CJ-1 about 0.44 N per kW of fuel heat: the electric engine is 27 times more efficient at turning energy into thrust. But the energy has to be stored, and propane holds 12.8 kWh per kilogram while a good lithium battery holds 0.18: 70 times less. Efficiency 27 times better, storage 70 times worse: the propane jet still wins the energy-per-thrust contest, by 2.6 times, and the gap widens the longer you fly.

What would flip the answer?Try the “battery specific energy” slider: at around 380 Wh/kg (about twice today’s cells) the battery mass for a 5-minute run falls below the propane jet’s fuel, bottle and regulator. That is why hobby RC jets are now mostly electric fans (short flights, no hot parts, no fuel handling), while airliners, which need hours, are not. Real engines also make sound and heat in other places: a ducted fan is less noisy at the exit than a turbojet but not silent.

Ion thrusters: acceleration by voltage

To go beyond 100 m/s of exhaust speed with a fan you would need ever more power; to reach tens of kilometres per second you need another approach: an electric field. Take a gas atom, strip an electron to make an ion with charge $q$, and let it fall through a voltage $V$. It gains kinetic energy $qV$, so

$$v=\sqrt{\frac{2qV}{m}},\qquad F=\dot m\,v=I\sqrt{\frac{2mV}{q}},\qquad I_{sp}=\frac{v}{g_0},\qquad P=\frac{IV}{\eta}$$

For xenon ($m=131$ u) at 1 100 V, $v=40$ km/s: an $I_{sp}$ of more than 4 000 s. Combine the equations: for a given power the thrust is $F/P=2\eta/v$. Speed and thrust per watt trade directly: double the exhaust speed and you halve the thrust you get for each kilowatt. An ion thruster of 2.5 kW gives about 100 mN, which is the weight of a coin or two.

The reason it cannot give more is the space-charge limit. Ions are all positive, and repel each other; a crowd of them between two grids shields the field and caps the current density (the Child–Langmuir law): $J_{max}=\tfrac49\varepsilon_0\sqrt{2q/m}\,V^{3/2}/d^2$. A smaller grid gap or a higher voltage lets more through (but at some point a spark between the grids ends the story: chapter 11’s breakdown once more). To raise the thrust you need a larger grid; to raise the Isp you need a higher voltage.

A neutraliser (a small electron emitter, usually a hollow cathode) sprays electrons into the beam. Without it the spacecraft would charge up negative and pull the ions straight back. The tiny thrust is not a handicap in space, where there is no drag: 90 mN on 500 kg is an acceleration of 0.18 mm/s², but it goes on for months, and months add up. NASA’s Dawn spacecraft used three ion engines to visit both Vesta and Ceres (2011–2018), a Δv of more than 11 km/s from 425 kg of xenon, which no chemical rocket of that size could have done.

Things to try

  1. Change the propellant: argon is cheap and light and gives a higher Isp for the same voltage, but needs more power per newton ($F/P=2\eta/v$): the cheap gas costs electricity.
  2. Raise the voltage to 3 000 V: Isp rises as $\sqrt V$ while the thrust at constant loading hardly changes; power climbs as $V^{3/2}$.
  3. Cut the available power to 1 kW: the controller throttles the beam current, and the thrust falls proportionally. This is how an ion engine behaves at Jupiter, where sunlight is 27 times weaker than at Earth.
  4. Look at the mission readouts: the same Δv with a chemical rocket (Isp 320 s) needs several times the propellant mass. The price is time: hundreds of days instead of minutes.

Ion wind: an engine with no moving parts and no tank

In air, a different kind of electric thruster is possible. A very thin wire at tens of kilovolts has such a strong field at its surface that it ionises the air next to it (a corona, the same process that makes a power line hiss on a damp day). The ions are pulled across a gap to a smooth collector, colliding with air molecules on the way and dragging them along: a silent wind with no fan blades. The ideal thrust is $F=I\,d/\mu$: current times gap divided by the ion mobility $\mu\approx2\times10^{-4}$ m²/(V·s). Real electrodes give about a third of that.

There is a window: below roughly 0.5 kV per millimetre of gap there is no corona; above about 1.4 kV per millimetre the corona turns into a spark (the breakdown of chapter 11, whose Paschen curve you have already met). Inside the window, thrust rises as the square of the voltage. The widget lets you explore that window and the central trade-off: a longer gap needs a higher voltage, so it gets harder to stay below sparking.

Safety: this is a calculator, not a build guideIon-wind demonstrations use 20–40 kV. The current is a milliampere, which is why it is not instantly lethal, but 30 kV can still give a painful and dangerous shock, a burn, and can be fatal for a person with a heart condition or a pacemaker; capacitors stay charged after switching off; corona makes ozone and nitrogen oxides; and sparks start fires. If you want to see the effect, do it in a school or university laboratory, under supervision, with a commercially built, current-limited supply, and never touch the electrodes. A real ion-wind aircraft exists: in 2018 an MIT team flew a 2.45 kg, five-metre aeroplane for 60 metres with no moving parts. The efficiency, around 2 N/kW, is a hundred times worse than a fan’s, which is why it is still a curiosity.

One map for every engine in the book

The three electric engines share one limit that no chemical engine has: power. The jet power of any engine is $P=\tfrac12FV_e=\tfrac12F\,I_{sp}\,g_0$, so thrust and Isp trade against each other for a given power. A chemical rocket burns propellant at a terrific rate: the Shuttle booster’s 12 MN at 2.4 km/s is 14 gigawatts of jet power from a tube of propellant. An ion thruster has the power of a couple of hair dryers. Even on its way to orbit with an ion engine at an Isp of 3 000 s, a one-tonne vehicle hovering against gravity needs $P=\tfrac12\times10^4\ \text{N}\times29\,400\ \text{m/s}=147$ MW, which is a solar array of more than a third of a square kilometre. That is the entire reason that lifting off a planet is done by chemistry, and getting around the solar system with electricity.

Look at where each of our engines sits: rockets at the right of centre in thrust, far from the power-limited electric region; the turbojet in between, with an Isp five to ten times a rocket’s because the oxidiser is free; the airliner turbofan farther up still, because its big bypass fan is exactly the same idea as this chapter’s ducted fan, driven by a gas turbine: a slow, fat jet is efficient.

What you now know

  • An electric engine separates energy (from a wire) from working fluid. Its limits are electrical power and the mass of the energy store.
  • Ducted fan: $F=(4\rho A_e)^{1/3}P^{2/3}$, and $F/P\approx2/V_e$. A big slow jet is efficient; thrust grows only as $P^{2/3}$.
  • The fan is 27× more efficient than the CJ-1 in turning energy into thrust, but a battery holds 70× less energy per kilogram than propane: propane wins by about 2.6× for a given run time.
  • Ion thruster: $v=\sqrt{2qV/m}$, $F=I\sqrt{2mV/q}$, $F/P=2\eta/v$. Exhaust at tens of km/s, thrust at tens of millinewtons, limited by the Child–Langmuir space-charge limit.
  • Ion wind: $F=\eta Id/\mu$, a narrow window between corona onset and spark-over; about 1–2 N/kW.
  • Jet power $P=\tfrac12FI_{sp}g_0$ ties thrust and Isp: chemical engines make gigawatts, electric ones kilowatts. That is why we use chemistry to leave the ground and electricity to travel between planets.
