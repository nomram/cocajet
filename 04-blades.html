Wings, fans & turbines

~80 min10 widgetsaerodynamics, turbomachinery, windmills, steam plants and fans

Our engine has exactly two moving parts that matter: a wheel that pushes air (the compressor) and a wheel that is pushed by air (the turbine). Both are rings of tiny wings. Understand one wing and you understand windmills, dams, power stations and jets.

How a wing makes lift

Stand in a river and hold a flat board at a slight angle: it is shoved sideways. A wing does the same with air, but smoothly. Three views of the same truth, all correct:

  • Newton: the wing deflects air downward; the air pushes the wing up (action and reaction, chapter 2).
  • Bernoulli: faster air has lower pressure. The curved upper surface speeds the flow, so pressure is lower on top than below: net upward force. $P+\tfrac12\rho v^2=\text{const}$ along a streamline.
  • Circulation: the flow around a wing is a uniform stream plus a circulation around the wing. The circulation is set by the sharp trailing edge (the “Kutta condition”: air must leave the rear edge smoothly). Lift per metre of span is $L=\rho V\Gamma$.

The key control is the angle of attack $\alpha$: the angle between the wing’s chord and the oncoming air. In the widget below the wind stays fixed (the arrow and the horizon do not move) and the wing itself tilts as you slide $\alpha$, so you can see what the angle means: the little aircraft in the corner pitches nose-up by the same amount. Try these, in this order:

  1. Nose up. Raise $\alpha$ from 0° to 8°. The streaks bend more sharply over the top, the pressure arrows on the upper surface grow, and the lift arrow lengthens. Press Replay: pitch up to watch the starting vortex: when the angle changes, a counter-rotating vortex peels off the trailing edge and drifts away. Because the total circulation of the air cannot change, the wing is left with an equal and opposite bound vortex: that circulation is the lift ($L'=\rho V\Gamma$).
  2. Switch on more forces. Each force chip adds an arrow with its value per metre of span: lift (perpendicular to the wind), drag (along it), their resultant, the same force split along and across the chord, the weight a level-flying wing would carry, the pressure on the skin, the skin friction and the centre of pressure, which creeps forward as $\alpha$ rises. Note how small the drag arrow is, and how large the lift: that ratio is the glide ratio.
  3. Stall it. Push $\alpha$ past about 15°. The separation point jumps forward, the lift falls, the drag climbs, and the wake becomes a street of alternating vortices.
  4. Make the wing finite. A real wing has tips. Open the aspect-ratio panel: air leaks from the high-pressure underside round each tip, trailing a wingtip vortex. The tip vortices push the air down behind the wing (downwash), which tilts the lift back and adds induced drag, $C_{Di}=C_L^2/(\pi\,AR\,e)$. A short stubby wing (AR 2) is hit hard; a glider’s long wing (AR 20) hardly at all. The same effect, at the tip of every compressor blade, is the tip-clearance loss of chapter 8.

Lift grows linearly with angle: thin-airfoil theory says $C_L\approx2\pi(\alpha-\alpha_0)$, about 0.11 per degree. Then, near 15°, comes the stall: the air can no longer follow the curve, it separates, lift collapses and drag soars. Every blade in our engine must stay below its stall angle at every radius and flow rate; when the compressor “surges” (chapter 7) it is a stalled wing.

$$L=\tfrac12\rho V^2 S\,C_L\qquad D=\tfrac12\rho V^2 S\,C_D\qquad \text{L/D is the "glide ratio"}$$

From wings to blades: why they are twisted

A fan blade is a wing that travels in a circle. The wind it sees is not the axial flow alone: it is the vector sum of the axial airspeed $C_a$ and the blade’s own motion $U=\omega r$. Far from the hub the blade moves faster, so the relative wind $W$ tilts further sideways. To keep the angle of attack right at every radius the blade must twist, steep near the hub, flat near the tip, exactly like a propeller.

$$W=\sqrt{C_a^2+U^2},\qquad \beta=\arctan\frac{U}{C_a},\qquad \text{incidence}=\beta-\beta_{\text{blade}}$$

Try our impeller’s inducer: 6 mm at the hub, 21 mm at the tip, 115 000 rpm. Switch to “flat” and watch the incidence go badly wrong.

Design rule you will use in chapter 6At the tip the relative speed is Mach 0.8–0.9. Push the tip speed higher and shocks form on the blades, losses explode, and the wheel “chokes”. The 56 mm wheel at 115 000 rpm has a tip speed of 337 m/s: close to that limit, and we can’t spin it much faster.

How much energy does a wheel give the air?

This is the single most important equation in turbomachinery. Take a ring of air of mass $\dot m$ per second entering at radius $r_1$ with swirl velocity $C_{\theta1}$ and leaving at $r_2$ with $C_{\theta2}$. The wheel exerts a torque equal to the change in angular momentum per second, and power is torque × angular speed:

$$\tau=\dot m\,(r_2C_{\theta2}-r_1C_{\theta1}),\quad P=\omega\tau\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta h_0=U_2C_{\theta2}-U_1C_{\theta1}$$

That is the Euler equation: the energy per kilogram (J/kg) given to the air equals tip speed × the swirl you induce. For a centrifugal wheel the air enters with no swirl ($C_{\theta1}=0$) so $\Delta h_0=U_2C_{\theta2}$. Two things limit $C_{\theta2}$:

  • Backsweep. If the blades lean back by $\beta_2$ from the radial direction the swirl is reduced: $C_{\theta2}=\sigma U_2 - C_{m2}\tan\beta_2$. Backswept blades give less pressure at design speed but a wider, calmer operating range.
  • Slip $\sigma$. The air doesn’t quite follow the blades: it lags, like water in a rotating bucket. Wiesner’s formula $\sigma=1-\dfrac{\sqrt{\cos\beta_2}}{Z^{0.7}}$ says more blades, less slip.

The map on the right shows the energy in the air as pressure ratio against rpm and wheel diameter. Each white dashed line is a constant tip speed. Everything scales with $U_2^2$: double the tip speed and you get four times the energy, which is why small engines have to spin at 100 000 rpm.

Our wheel, by hand$U_2=\pi D N/60=\pi(0.056)(115\,000)/60=337$ m/s. $\sigma=1-\sqrt{\cos30^\circ}/(0.85\times14)^{0.7}=0.836$, $C_{m2}=0.28\,U_2=94$ m/s, so $C_{\theta2}=0.836(337)-94\tan30^\circ=227$ m/s and $\Delta h_0=337\times227=76$ kJ/kg (+ some disc friction: ~79 kJ/kg of shaft work). With 77 % efficiency, $PR=(1+0.77\times76\,000/(1005\times288))^{3.5}\approx1.9$. (The full engine model also counts disc friction and recirculation work and gives 1.94: the number the whole guide uses.)

Reverse the machine: wind turbines

A turbine takes energy out of the fluid. Everything is the same, backwards: the relative wind hits the blade, lift is generated, and its component along the rotation drives the shaft. In chapter 2 we found the wind carries $\tfrac12\rho v^3$ watts per m². How much can a rotor take?

$$P=\tfrac12\rho A v^3\,C_p,\qquad C_p\le\tfrac{16}{27}=0.593\quad\text{(Betz limit)}$$
Where does the Betz limit come from?

Let the wind slow to $(1-a)v$ at the rotor and to $(1-2a)v$ far behind it ($a$ = induction factor). Momentum theory gives the force $F=2\rho A v^2a(1-a)$ and so the power $P=Fv(1-a)=2\rho Av^3a(1-a)^2$. Divide by the wind power $\tfrac12\rho Av^3$:

$$C_p=4a(1-a)^2\qquad\frac{dC_p}{da}=0\Rightarrow a=\tfrac13\Rightarrow C_p=\tfrac{16}{27}\approx0.593$$

If the rotor took more it would slow the air so much that it would pile up in front and flow around instead.

Real rotors reach 0.45–0.50. The tip-speed ratio $\lambda=\omega R/v$ (blade-tip speed divided by wind speed) matters too: the blades must keep the right angle of attack on the wind they see, so a rotor is built for one design tip-speed ratio $\lambda_d$, and its chord and twist follow from that choice.

How many blades? Set the blade count anywhere from 1 to 12, with $\lambda_d=7$. One blade is cheap but wobbles and loses a lot of air around its tip; going to two adds about 0.08 to $C_p$, to three another 0.03, to four only about 0.01, and every blade after that gains almost nothing. The total blade area barely changes (the chord of each blade shrinks as $1/B$): you are chopping the same area into more thin, flimsy, expensive pieces with more bearings and more noise. So three is the standard: balanced, smooth, near-optimal. Now slide the design tip-speed ratio down to 1–2. A slow rotor must turn slow air into torque, so it needs wide blades covering most of the disc, and many of them: with $\lambda_d=1$ three blades manage $C_p\approx0.2$ and twelve $C_p\approx0.34$. Windmills old and new: drag versus lift

The wind turbine is the latest member of a very old family. Persian vertical-axis mills ground grain a thousand years ago; the Dutch sail mill (four lattice sails, from the Middle Ages) drained polders and sawed timber; the American farm windmill (12–24 curved blades behind a tail vane, 1850s on) pumped water for cattle across the prairies; and the modern two- or three-blade turbine makes electricity. Each shape is a different answer to the same question: what load does the shaft drive, and how fast must it turn?

$$\lambda=\frac{\omega R}{v},\qquad P=\tfrac12\rho A v^3\,C_p,\qquad C_{p}\le\tfrac{16}{27},\qquad \sigma=\frac{\text{blade area}}{\text{swept area}}$$

The key divide is drag-type versus lift-type. A drag device (a cup, a paddle, the Savonius “S”) is pushed by the wind like a sail on a cart. The push is $F=\tfrac12\rho C_D S\,(v-u)^2$, where $u$ is the speed of the paddle, so it vanishes when the paddle catches up with the wind, and a drag rotor can never turn faster than the wind ($\lambda<1$). Power $P=Fu$ peaks at $u=v/3$:

$$C_p\le\frac{4}{27}\,C_D\approx0.15\,C_D\;\Rightarrow\;C_p\lesssim0.2\text{ for real paddles}$$

A lift-type blade is a wing. It sees the wind and the wind created by its own motion, $w=\sqrt{v^2+u^2}$, so at $\lambda=7$ it feels seven times the real wind, and lift grows with $w^2$. That is how a thin 3-blade rotor with 4 % solidity beats a solid Savonius, and why only lift machines approach Betz. It is the same trick as the compressor blade in the Euler equation: the blade’s own speed does the work.

The design $\lambda_d$ is set by the load. Pumping water with a piston needs a lot of torque $Q=P/\omega$ at a few revolutions per minute, even from a faint breeze: use many wide blades (solidity $\sigma$ of 60–80 % of the disc) and a low $\lambda_d\approx1$. A generator wants the opposite, a high shaft speed so the gearbox and the generator stay small: use few slender blades ($\sigma\approx4$ %) and $\lambda_d\approx7$–$9$. Steam turbines: coal, gas and nuclear power plants

A nuclear reactor is just a very expensive kettle. Fission heats water to steam at ~285 °C and 70 bar; the steam spins a turbine (a many-stage bladed wheel, the same physics as ours) which turns a generator; the exhaust steam is condensed back to water in a cooling tower or river, pumped back to pressure, and the loop repeats. That is the Rankine cycle: like Brayton, but the working fluid is boiled and condensed instead of burnt and discarded. Its efficiency is capped by the Carnot limit $\eta\le1-T_c/T_h$, which is why hotter is better and why gas turbines, which start 1000 °C hotter, rule the top of the table:

The family treeOur engine = a gas turbine used as a jet. A turboshaft engine (helicopters, power stations) adds a second turbine to drive a shaft instead of making a jet. A turbofan adds a big front fan. A combined-cycle plant uses the hot exhaust of a gas turbine to boil water for a steam turbine, achieving 60 % efficiency, the best of any heat engine. It’s all Brayton plus Rankine plus blades.

Steam power plants: the Rankine cycle up close

Burn coal, split uranium, focus sunlight, tap a hot spring: however the heat is made, a conventional power station then does one thing with it. It boils water. The steam spins a turbine, the turbine spins a generator, and the steam is turned back into water so it can be boiled again. Four machines, one closed loop (the numbers are the state points in the widget below):

  1. Pump (3→4). Condensed water at 0.01 MPa is squeezed up to boiler pressure, 10 MPa in our default plant.
  2. Boiler (4→1). Heat from the flame, reactor or mirrors warms the water, boils it at constant pressure and superheats the steam to 500 °C.
  3. Turbine (1→2). The steam expands back down to condenser pressure and does work. A perfect turbine would expand at constant entropy to $h_{2s}$; a real one achieves only $\eta_T=(h_1-h_2)/(h_1-h_{2s})$ of that.
  4. Condenser (2→3). River, sea or cooling-tower water takes the exhaust heat away and the steam condenses at constant temperature (46 °C at 10 kPa, a vacuum at one tenth of an atmosphere).
$$w_T=h_1-h_2,\qquad w_P=\frac{v\,(p_b-p_c)}{\eta_P},\qquad q_{in}=h_1-h_4,\qquad \eta_{th}=\frac{w_T-w_P}{q_{in}}$$

Why the pump costs almost nothing

The whole design rests on one trick. The pump moves liquid, and liquid is almost incompressible, so raising its pressure takes $w_P=\int v\,dp\approx v\,\Delta p=0.001\ \text{m}^3/\text{kg}\times10^{7}\ \text{Pa}=10$ kJ/kg. The turbine returns about 1 285 kJ/kg for the same pressure drop. Had we tried to compress the same steam as a gas from 10 kPa up to 10 MPa, it would have cost roughly 2 450 kJ/kg, more than the turbine can give back. So the cycle condenses first, pumps the liquid, then boils it again at high pressure. The back-work ratio (compressor or pump work divided by turbine work) is about 1 % for steam.

Brayton versus RankineIn a gas turbine the compressor works on a gas, so it swallows 40–60 % of all the work the hot gas can deliver (52 % for CJ-1’s cycle in chapter 3). Lose a few points of blade efficiency and the net work vanishes. A steam plant keeps 99 % of its turbine work, and it can forgive a mediocre turbine. That is why Parsons’ steam turbine ran in 1884 and the first useful gas turbines had to wait until 1939.

Superheat, reheat and supercritical: climbing the temperature ladder

The efficiency of any heat engine is set by the temperatures at which heat enters and leaves. For a steam plant the heat does not enter at one temperature: the water is first warmed from 46 °C to its boiling point, then boiled, then superheated. What counts is the mean temperature of heat addition $\bar T_h$, the average of $T$ over the entropy gained in the boiler. Every lever below raises $\bar T_h$ or lowers $T_c$:

  • Hotter steam (superheat). Efficiency climbs, and the exhaust is drier. The limit is metallurgy: creep (chapter 9) caps steel boiler tubes at about 600–620 °C; nickel alloys for 700 °C plants are still being developed. This is the same wall the turbojet’s turbine hits.
  • Higher pressure. Water boils hotter, so $\bar T_h$ rises. The price: the expansion now ends further inside the dome. At 540 °C and 10 kPa, a 2 MPa boiler gives exhaust that is 99 % dry, a 10 MPa boiler 90 %, a 25 MPa boiler only 82 %.
  • Reheat. Expand the steam down to roughly a fifth of boiler pressure, send it back to the boiler to be heated to the same temperature again, and expand the rest. It dries the exhaust and adds heat at high temperature.
  • Supercritical. Above 22.06 MPa and 374 °C there is no boiling at all: the dome ends, and water changes smoothly from dense to light without ever forming bubbles. At 25–30 MPa and 600 °C the steam cycle approaches 45–47 % in a real plant.
  • Feed-water heating (regeneration, not in the widget). Bleeding a little steam out of the turbine to pre-heat the water entering the boiler means the boiler never has to heat cold water. Real plants have six to nine such heaters and gain 4–6 points, which is why the widget’s numbers sit below the “typical” bars on the comparison chart.
The 0.88 rule: wet steam eats bladesWater droplets in the last turbine rows hit the blades at several hundred m/s. Designers keep the steam at least 88 % dry at the turbine exit (quality $x\ge0.88$); below that the blade tips erode within months. Reheat exists mostly to satisfy this limit and the efficiency gain is a bonus.
Why does the loop area equal the work, and what is the mean temperature?

On a T–s diagram, $q=\int T\,ds$ is the area under a process. Heat in is the area under the boiler line, heat out the (smaller) area under the condenser line, and the first law says the work out is the difference: the enclosed area. Define $\bar T_h=q_{in}/\Delta s$, the height of a rectangle with the same area under the heating curve. For a reversible cycle the condenser line is at $T_c$ over the same $\Delta s$, so

$$\eta=\frac{q_{in}-q_{out}}{q_{in}}=1-\frac{T_c\,\Delta s}{\bar T_h\,\Delta s}=1-\frac{T_c}{\bar T_h}$$

Our ideal 10 MPa / 500 °C / 10 kPa plant has $q_{in}=3\,173$ kJ/kg and $\Delta s=6.599-0.649=5.95$ kJ/(kg·K). So $\bar T_h=533$ K (260 °C, far below the 500 °C steam!) and $\eta=1-319/533=40.2\%$. Most of the heat goes in while the water is still being warmed or boiled at lower temperatures; that is the loss that regeneration, high pressure and reheat all attack.

Where the waste heat goes

The second law demands that most of the heat leave through the condenser. For 500 MW of electricity from the default plant that is about 970 MW, nearly twice the electrical output, and it must be carried away by cooling water. Heating 23 m³/s of river water by 10 K does it: a small river. Where there is no river, the plant uses a cooling tower, in which warm water trickles down through rising air and a share of it evaporates; each kilogram carries off 2.26 MJ, so 970 MW needs about 430 kg/s (some 37 000 m³ per day). The white plume above a cooling tower is not smoke but a cloud of condensed water droplets.

The condenser temperature sets the back-pressure, and a colder sink is worth real efficiency: this is why plants like cold rivers and winter nights. Run the same plant on the opposite principle and you get combined heat and power (CHP): the steam is allowed to leave the turbine at 1 bar or higher, the condenser becomes a 100 °C hot-water supply for a district-heating network, and 80–90 % of the fuel energy is used, even though the electrical efficiency drops.

Gas turbine plus steam turbine: the combined cycle

Take the top of the engine in this guide and put a bottoming cycle under it. A gas-turbine power plant is a jet engine whose exhaust drives a power turbine and a generator instead of a nozzle. Remove CJ-1’s nozzle, add a second turbine behind the first, couple it to a generator, and you have a very small, very inefficient one. Real ones are the same machine at 100× the scale: the GE LM2500, which powers frigates and offshore platforms, is the core of the CF6 airliner engine with a power turbine on the back. The exhaust of such a turbine still leaves at 550–650 °C and holds well over half of the fuel’s energy. A heat-recovery steam generator (a bank of finned tubes in the exhaust duct) boils water with it, and a steam turbine makes extra electricity from heat that would otherwise be thrown into the sky. The Brayton cycle of chapter 3 on top of a Rankine cycle: the first rejects its heat at 600 °C, exactly the temperature at which the second wants to receive it.

$$\eta_{cc}=\eta_{gt}+(1-\eta_{gt})\,\varepsilon\,\eta_{st}$$

The gas turbine converts $\eta_{gt}$ of the fuel to electricity; the remaining $(1-\eta_{gt})$ leaves in the exhaust; the heat-recovery boiler hands a fraction $\varepsilon$ (its effectiveness) of that to a steam cycle that converts $\eta_{st}$ of it. Plug in $0.38+0.62\times0.75\times0.35=54\%$ with a 35 % steam cycle like the simple plant you designed; a three-pressure steam cycle with reheat and a better boiler ($\eta_{st}=0.40$, $\varepsilon=0.85$) gives 59 %, and the best machines, with 42 % gas turbines, exceed 62 %. The widget’s Compare plants tab lets you change all three numbers and shows where each plant sits relative to the Carnot ceiling of the steam cycle. Our own CJ-1 is at the bottom: its jet carries 11 kW of kinetic energy for 132 kW of propane, about 8 %. At zero airspeed that jet does no useful propulsive work at all, and a power turbine behind the first would convert much of it into shaft power; this is exactly what turns a jet into a power plant.

Fans: the compressor that hardly compresses

A fan is a compressor with the pressure ratio turned almost all the way down: $p_2/p_1$ between 1.001 and about 1.1. A PC fan raises the pressure of the air by 20–50 Pa (a ratio of 1.0003); a kitchen extractor by 100–300 Pa; an industrial blower by up to 10 kPa. CJ-1’s compressor makes 1.94, and an airliner’s compressor stack more than 40. Because the density changes by under 7 % even for the biggest fan, air can be treated as incompressible: no temperature rise to keep track of, and the simple scaling laws below are exact. A fan adds pressure; a compressor adds enthalpy. The Euler equation still describes the wheel: $\Delta p=\eta\,\rho\,U_2C_{\theta2}$.

Three quantities describe any fan:

$$\dot m=\rho Q,\qquad P_{air}=Q\,\Delta p,\qquad \eta=\frac{P_{air}}{P_{shaft}}$$

Move 2 m³/s of air against 500 Pa and the air receives $2\times500=1$ kW. If the motor must supply 1.4 kW, the fan is 71 % efficient. The pressure the fan can deliver falls as the flow rises (the fan curve); the pressure the duct demands rises with flow. Turbulent loss is proportional to velocity squared, so the system curve is a parabola, $\Delta p=KQ^2$, and the fan runs where the two cross. Close a damper and $K$ goes up, the crossing slides up the fan curve, and the flow drops.

Look closely at the left end of the axial fan’s curve: it has a dip. Reduce the flow at constant speed and the axial velocity $C_a$ falls, so the relative flow angle $\beta=\arctan(U/C_a)$ steepens and the incidence on every blade grows (the wing of chapter 4’s first section). Past the stall angle the air separates from the blades, the curve turns over, and a fan working there surges between two flows with a roar. Forward-curved fans have a milder dip; backward-curved fans have almost none. It is the same stall that produces compressor surge in chapter 7.

The affinity laws

Turn a fan faster or build it bigger without changing its shape, and every velocity in the flow scales with the blade speed $U=\pi DN/60$ while the angles stay the same. Flow is velocity times area, pressure goes as $\rho U^2$, and power is their product:

$$\frac{Q_2}{Q_1}=\frac{N_2}{N_1}\left(\frac{D_2}{D_1}\right)^{3},\qquad \frac{\Delta p_2}{\Delta p_1}=\left(\frac{N_2}{N_1}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{D_2}{D_1}\right)^{2},\qquad \frac{P_2}{P_1}=\left(\frac{N_2}{N_1}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{D_2}{D_1}\right)^{5}$$

The cube law is the one worth remembering. Cut the speed by 20 % ($N_2/N_1=0.8$) and the fan still delivers 80 % of the air, at $0.8^2=64\%$ of the pressure, but needs only $0.8^3=51\%$ of the power: a saving of 49 %. That is why variable-speed drives are the standard way to control big ventilation fans. Closing a damper to get the same 80 % flow barely helps: in the widget the shaft power stays within a few percent of its previous value (94–101 %, depending on the fan type), because the fan is still spinning at full speed and just burning the pressure in a restriction. Doubling the diameter at the same speed does the opposite: eight times the flow, four times the pressure, thirty-two times the power.

Specific speed: which kind of fan should this be?

Combine the three laws to cancel the size and speed and you get one dimensionless number that depends only on the shape of the machine, the specific speed

$$\omega_s=\frac{\omega\sqrt{Q}}{(\Delta p/\rho)^{3/4}}\qquad(\omega\text{ in rad/s, }Q\text{ in m}^3\text{/s})$$

A low $\omega_s$ (below about 1.8) means high pressure and modest flow: a centrifugal wheel. A high $\omega_s$ (above about 3.5) means big flow and low pressure: an axial wheel. In between lies the mixed-flow fan. The same number explains why CJ-1’s one-stage compressor is centrifugal and its turbine axial (see the next section). The readout in the widget stays put when you change the speed or the diameter, and it moves only when the operating point slides along the curve.

The last row is the one that connects back to propulsion. A turbofan is a ducted fan driven by a gas turbine, with a fan pressure ratio of only about 1.5 (a “fan”, not a compressor) and a bypass ratio of 5–12. The thrust is $F=\dot m(V_e-V_0)$, and the kinetic energy thrown away behind the engine per second is $\tfrac12\dot m(V_e-V_0)^2$. For the same thrust, a bigger $\dot m$ means a smaller $V_e-V_0$ and so much less wasted energy. Put as a number, the propulsive efficiency is

$$\eta_p=\frac{F\,V_0}{F V_0+\tfrac12\dot m(V_e-V_0)^2}=\frac{2}{1+V_e/V_0}$$

At an airliner’s 250 m/s a turbojet with a 600 m/s jet has $\eta_p=2/(1+2.4)=0.59$; a high-bypass turbofan whose fan stream leaves at 320 m/s has $\eta_p=2/(1+1.28)=0.88$. The same core, the same fuel, nearly half again as much propulsion. Chapter 2 explained why big and slow beats small and fast, chapter 8 bolts a fan onto CJ-1 in the bypass widget, and chapter 15 does the same with electricity. The fan in front of a jet engine is the fan on your desk, scaled up and driven by a turbine.

What you now know

  • A wing makes lift in proportion to $\alpha$ until it stalls near 15°. Compressor surge = a stalled blade row.
  • A rotating blade sees the vector sum of axial speed and blade speed, so it must be twisted.
  • Euler: $\Delta h_0=U_2C_{\theta2}$. Energy goes with tip speed squared; slip and backsweep reduce the swirl; ~14 blades at exit is a good compromise.
  • Turbines reverse the same physics: wind (Betz ≤ 59 %), hydro ($P=\eta\rho gQH$), steam (Carnot-limited).
  • Wind rotors are lift machines: few slender blades at tip-speed ratio $\lambda\approx7$–$9$ reach $C_p\approx0.45$–$0.50$, extra blades gain little, and a drag-type rotor (Savonius) is stuck near $C_p\approx0.2$ because a paddle cannot outrun the wind.
  • The load picks the windmill: water pumping wants many wide blades, low $\lambda$ and huge starting torque; a generator wants few blades and a fast shaft.
  • A steam plant boils, expands, condenses and pumps (Rankine). The pump moves liquid, so it needs about 1 % of the turbine work against 40–60 % for a gas turbine’s compressor; reheat and supercritical pressure raise efficiency and keep the exit steam above 88 % dry.
  • A fan is a compressor with $p_2/p_1\le1.1$: $Q\propto ND^3$, $\Delta p\propto N^2D^2$, $P\propto N^3D^5$, so 20 % less speed saves 49 % of the power. Gas turbine + steam turbine gives $\eta_{cc}=\eta_{gt}+(1-\eta_{gt})\,\varepsilon\,\eta_{st}\approx60$ %.
  • CJ-1 pairs a one-stage centrifugal compressor with a one-stage axial turbine.
