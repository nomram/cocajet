Chapter 16 · Classes of rockets

The rocket zoo: what the classes are

~70 min5 widgetsfrom a water bottle to Starship, and up versus sideways

A water rocket and a moon rocket obey the same equations, but their lift-off masses differ by a factor of ten million, and so does almost everything else about them: who may fly them, what motors they use, what they can carry and what they cost. People therefore sort rockets in several ways at once. This chapter lays out the sorting systems one by one (by motor size, by the rules, by job, by payload, by engine and architecture) and shows the one physical quantity, the thrust-to-weight ratio, that quietly decides which class a rocket must belong to.

Sort by motor size: the letters A to O

Chapter 13 introduced the label on a hobby motor. The letter is the total impulse, $I=\int F\,dt$, in newton-seconds, and each letter allows twice the impulse of the one before. That makes the ladder logarithmic, like the Richter scale: from an A motor to an O motor is fourteen doublings, a factor of more than 16 000.

For reference: the CJ-1 at full power for one minute delivers 3 500 N·s, an L-class impulse, but it delivers it as 58 N for 60 s instead of 1 000 N for 3 s. The class tells you the energy-like total, not how hard the push is; the second number in the label (the average thrust) and the burn time tell the rest. Notice what the ladder buys you: the Level system is a training path. To fly H and I motors you first prove you can build, fly and recover a rocket on smaller ones; to move up to J, K and L you do it again; and so on. It is the same logic as an aircraft licence, and the reason the hobby has an excellent safety record under clubs and a poor one outside them.

Staying on the right side of the lineThe categories exist because the hazard rises steeply with impulse: the energy in an O motor is thousands of times that in a C motor, and the distance at which a failure can hurt someone grows with it. Use certified motors from a seller who checks your certification, follow the club’s distance tables, and never modify a motor or build propellant at home (chapter 13). This chapter says nothing about weapons; military missiles share the physics but are outside this guide.

The classes are rough: the same rocket changes class depending on how much it saves for recovery, and the numbers shift between versions. The point is the pattern. Look at the chart and notice that the class boundaries line up with lift-off mass (a medium launcher weighs hundreds of tonnes, a super-heavy one thousands), and that the whole zoo falls along a band of constant thrust-to-weight ratio between 1.2 and 1.5, far below the hobby rockets.

Inside the giants: how big the tanks are

Strip the paint off a launcher and what you find is mostly tank. In the five vehicles below, 85–92 % of the lift-off mass is propellant, roughly 5–10 % is structure (tanks, engines, interstages) and only 1–5 % is payload. A rocket is a long, thin-walled pressure vessel with engines bolted to one end, so its shape is decided by the volume of its propellant, and that volume is decided by the propellant’s density.

Density sets the shape

Two facts fix a liquid stage’s tanks. The engine burns oxidiser and fuel in a fixed ratio by mass, $r=m_{ox}/m_f$ (from public engine fact sheets: about 2.3 for kerosene with liquid oxygen, about 3.6 for methane, 5–6 for hydrogen), and each liquid has its own density. For a stage with propellant mass $m_p$:

$$V_{ox}=\frac{r}{1+r}\,\frac{m_p}{\rho_{ox}},\qquad V_f=\frac{1}{1+r}\,\frac{m_p}{\rho_f},\qquad \frac{V_f}{V_{ox}}=\frac{\rho_{ox}}{r\,\rho_f}$$

The cold liquids weigh: oxygen 1 141 kg/m³, kerosene (RP-1) ≈ 810, methane ≈ 423, hydrogen only ≈ 71. So the fuel tank is smaller than the oxygen tank for kerosene ($V_f/V_{ox}\approx0.6$) and for methane (≈ 0.75), but for hydrogen it comes out the other way round: the oxygen weighs five to six times as much as the hydrogen, and the hydrogen tank is nearly three times the size of the oxygen tank. The Shuttle’s external tank holds 629 t of oxygen in about 550 m³ and 106 t of hydrogen in about 1 500 m³ (the widget reproduces the published tank volumes to within a percent), and that is why it is the fat orange thing the orbiter hangs from. Compare two Saturn V stages of the same 10.1 m diameter: the kerosene first stage carries about 51 t of propellant per metre of its length, the hydrogen second stage about 18 t per metre.

Averaged over both tanks the propellant weighs ≈ 1 010 kg/m³ for kerosene and oxygen, ≈ 830 for methane and oxygen, ≈ 350 for hydrogen and oxygen, and ≈ 1 750 for a cast solid. A fluffy propellant needs more tank volume, hence more tank wall, hence more dead weight for every tonne it holds: that is the price of hydrogen’s high Isp (452 s in vacuum for the Shuttle’s engines against 311 s for Falcon 9’s kerosene engines; chapter 14 draws the trade). It pays on an upper stage, whose structure is, as far as every stage below it is concerned, payload. It does not pay on a first stage, which needs a lot of thrust from a compact rocket: that is why Saturn V’s first stage is the slim, heavy one and its upper stages are the voluminous ones. Tanks also share walls: in the S-II and S-IVB the oxygen and hydrogen tanks are separated by a single insulated common bulkhead, a dome that is the top of one tank and the bottom of the other, which saves a whole dome of structure.

It is a check, not a proof: Saturn V’s total counts only the S-IVB’s first burn (to orbit) and not the Moon burn that follows, the recovered boosters of Falcon 9 and Starship keep propellant back for landing, and Starship is flagged “approximate” in the widget because its masses and thrust change with every version.

In series and in parallel

Saturn V and Falcon 9 are stacked in series: each stage ignites after the one below has dropped away, and carries only what is above it. The Shuttle and Soyuz use parallel staging: boosters (two solid ones for the Shuttle, four kerosene ones for Soyuz) and the core stage all light on the pad, the boosters drop away after about two minutes, and the core keeps burning from tanks it has hardly emptied. The advantage is a big lift-off thrust from engines that are not dragged all the way to orbit: the Shuttle’s two boosters give four fifths of its 30 MN. The price shows in the structure fraction $\varepsilon$, the empty mass over empty-plus-propellant mass: ≈ 15 % for a Shuttle booster, ≈ 3.5 % for its tank. A solid motor has to be its own pressure vessel for hot gas at tens of atmospheres, whereas a liquid tank holds cold propellant at low pressure and lets the engine, a separate part, do the hot work. (A solid propellant is fuel, oxidiser of the ammonium perchlorate class and a binder, cast as one block in the case; this guide stops there, see chapter 13.)

Why Starship burns methane

Methane with oxygen sits between kerosene and hydrogen on every score the widget shows. Its vacuum Isp (≈ 350–380 s for the Raptor engine) is better than kerosene’s; its propellant is far denser than hydrogen’s (≈ 830 kg/m³ against ≈ 350), so a given mass needs about 20 % more tank volume than kerosene, not almost three times as much; the two liquids are cold at similar temperatures (oxygen at about −183 °C, methane at about −162 °C, hydrogen at −253 °C), so they can share a bulkhead without a thick insulating layer between them; it burns cleanly, without the soot kerosene leaves in an engine that must be reused; and it can in principle be made from carbon dioxide and water on Mars. For a vehicle meant to fly again and again, those count for more than the last 80 s of Isp.

Could one stage do it all?

The last panel of the widget runs the rocket equation backwards: $m_f/m_0=e^{-\Delta v/(I_{sp}g_0)}$. A stage with structure fraction $\varepsilon$ and payload fraction $\lambda$ needs $m_0/m_f=1/(\varepsilon+\lambda(1-\varepsilon))$, so the payload fraction reaches zero at $\varepsilon=e^{-\Delta v/(I_{sp}g_0)}$. For 9.4 km/s that limit is ≈ 5.5 % at an effective Isp of 330 s (kerosene) and ≈ 10 % at 420 s (hydrogen). The real stages in the table sit between 3.5 % and 9 %: the Shuttle tank (3.5 %), Falcon 9’s second stage (3.6 %), Saturn V’s first stage (5.7 %), Falcon 9’s first stage (6.1 %), the S-II (7.4 %). That is why every orbital rocket flown so far has two or more stages, or drops a booster: a single stage with kerosene would have to be lighter than the lightest of them while also carrying engines and a payload, and a hydrogen one leaves a payload of a few percent at best.

The hidden boundary: thrust-to-weight ratio

There is one number that separates the hobby rocket from the orbital launcher, and it is not size: it is the lift-off thrust-to-weight ratio $T/W=F/(mg)$. A rocket accelerates at $(T/W-1)\,g$; at $T/W=1$ it does not even leave the pad. Why would a launcher not simply use a higher $T/W$? Look at the burn time for a fixed mass ratio $R$ and specific impulse $I_{sp}$:

$$t_b=\frac{(1-1/R)\,I_{sp}}{T/W},\qquad \Delta v_{\text{gravity}}\approx g\,t_b$$

The longer the burn, the longer gravity pulls the rocket back. With $I_{sp}=300$ s and $R=4$: at $T/W=1.3$ the burn takes 173 s and gravity eats 1.7 km/s; at $T/W=5$ it is 45 s and 440 m/s; at 15, 150 m/s. So a bigger $T/W$ is better for the speed, and the price is paid elsewhere: more engines, stronger structure, and more drag low in the dense air (the rocket is fastest when the air is thickest). The launchers’ compromise is $T/W\approx1.2$–$1.5$, and they then tilt over (a “gravity turn”) so that later in the climb gravity works against the horizontal velocity less.

A hobby rocket sits at the other end for a different reason. It has no guidance, only fins, and fins steer only if the air flows past them quickly. Clubs therefore ask for a thrust-to-weight of about 5 or more at lift-off and a speed of at least about 15 m/s leaving the launch rod. Why a big rocket is easier than a small one

Compare a model rocket and a moon rocket and you find one of the oldest rules in engineering, the square-cube law. If you scale a rocket up by a factor $L$ in every dimension, its propellant volume (and mass) grows as $L^3$ but its tank surface, and so the mass of a thin tank wall, grows only as $L^2$. The structure fraction $\varepsilon$ falls as the rocket grows, which, through the rocket equation’s logarithm, gives a better mass ratio for the same technology. Drag works the same way: the ballistic coefficient $m/(C_dA)$ grows as $L$, so a large rocket loses far less of its speed to the air than a small one. A hobby rocket can lose a quarter of its speed to drag; a launcher usually loses only a few hundred metres per second out of 9 400. The catch is that big rockets also have bigger problems (many engines in a small space, bigger tanks to cool and to keep from buckling) so each class needs its own engineering, not just a magnification.

What can a jet engine carry? Up versus sideways

A jet engine makes thrust, not lift, so how much it can “carry” depends on what that thrust has to push against. Take the CJ-1 and its 58 N and try both ways.

Straight up, thrust pushes directly against weight: $F-mg=ma$, so the heaviest load the engine can hold up or accelerate upward at $a$ is

$$m_{max}=\frac{F}{g+a}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad m_{hover}=\frac{F}{g}=\frac{58\ \text{N}}{9.81\ \text{m/s}^2}\approx5.9\ \text{kg}$$

and 1.1 kg of that is the engine itself: about 4.8 kg is left for tank, fuel, airframe and anything you want to carry. Sideways, the wing does the lifting. In steady level flight lift equals weight and thrust equals drag, so the engine only has to beat the drag, which is the weight divided by the lift-to-drag ratio $L/D$:

$$T=D=\frac{W}{L/D}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad m_{max}=\frac{T\,(L/D)}{g}\qquad\text{e.g.}\quad \frac{58\times10}{9.81}\approx59\ \text{kg}$$

The same engine keeps $L/D$ times more mass flying than it can hold hovering. That is the sense in which an aeroplane is a lift multiplier: the wing leans on the air, and the engine only pays the toll. A model-jet airframe manages $L/D\approx8$–12, an airliner 17–19, an efficient sailplane over 40 (with no engine at all). And $L/D$ is a property of the airframe, not a slider: with the parabolic drag polar $C_D=C_{D0}+C_L^2/(\pi\,AR\,e)$ the best value is

$$\left(\frac LD\right)_{max}=\frac12\sqrt{\frac{\pi\,AR\,e}{C_{D0}}},\qquad\text{flown at}\quad C_L=\sqrt{\pi\,AR\,e\,C_{D0}}$$

so a long, slim wing (high aspect ratio $AR$) and a clean, smooth airframe (low $C_{D0}$) are what buy the multiplier. The price of the multiplier is speed. The wing stalls below the speed at which it would need more than its maximum lift coefficient, $V_s=\sqrt{2W/(\rho S C_{L,max})}$, so a sideways “carrier” needs a runway, a catapult or a launch, and thrust to spare to get up to speed; the wing and tail are also weight that a hovering machine would not carry. The widget’s two modes show both sides.

Height: thrust falls, the minimum thrust needed does not

A jet’s thrust comes from the air it swallows, so it falls with the air’s density; a good rule of thumb is $F\approx F_0\,(\rho/\rho_0)^n$ with $n$ between 0.7 and 0.9, and a loss as flight speed rises. At 8–10 km, where the density is 43–34 % of the sea-level value, the CJ-1 has only 47–38 % of its thrust (the dots in the widget come from this guide’s own engine model and sit on the curve), and an airliner’s turbofans at cruise Mach have about a fifth. The thrust needed behaves differently. The minimum drag in level flight is $W/(L/D)_{max}$ at any height: thin air does not make the aircraft heavier, it only raises the best speed (as $1/\sqrt\rho$), because the wing must be pushed faster through thin air to make the same lift. So the service ceiling is where the falling thrust available meets that fixed minimum: in the second chart, where the orange curve (the heaviest aircraft the engine can keep flying) crosses your aircraft’s weight. For a 25 kg airframe with the CJ-1 that is at about 9 km; for a 70 t airliner on two CFM56-class engines it is at about 13 km, close to what airliners do.

Two more things close in before the thrust gives out, and the widget includes both. The stall speed rises with height as $1/\sqrt\rho$, while the speed at which the wing’s drag starts to climb steeply (the critical Mach number, about 0.78 for an airliner) stays put. The window between “too slow to fly” and “too fast for the wing” therefore narrows with height until it closes; pilots call that last stretch the coffin corner. Any real design needs far more than this: it ignores climb, take-off, engine installation, control and the weather.

The rocket for contrast

The rocket on the widget’s engine list is a Merlin-class engine of 845 kN, and it behaves in just the opposite way. Its thrust does not depend on the air at all (it even rises by about 8 % in vacuum, as the nozzle stops fighting the ambient pressure), so the mass it can hold hovering, 86 t, hardly changes with height. But it carries its own oxidiser: it burns 7 to 30 times as much propellant per newton as the jets do (an Isp of 280–310 s against 2 070 s for the CJ-1 and roughly 5 500 s for a turbofan in cruise), and a tank that big on a hovering rocket is empty in about a minute. The jet’s free oxidiser, the air, is what lets it fly for hours; the rocket’s independence of the air is what lets it leave the atmosphere. Two ideas, two uses.

What you now know

  • Motor classes A–O are letters for total impulse; each letter doubles the one before, and the second number in the label is the average thrust.
  • Rules follow the letters: model rockets (up to G, 160 N·s, up to 125 g of propellant, up to 1 500 g in total in the US); high-power H–O with club certification Levels 1–3 (H–I, J–L, M–O); anything above 40 960 N·s is a waiver-level “Class 3”.
  • By job: sounding rockets (suborbital), orbital launchers, upper stages, spacecraft thrusters.
  • Launch vehicles are classed by payload to LEO: small up to 2 t, medium 2–20 t, heavy 20–50 t, super-heavy above 50 t.
  • Δv sets the class: 1.5–2 km/s to the edge of space, 9.4 km/s to low orbit, then about 3 km/s more for the Moon, geostationary orbit or escape.
  • Thrust-to-weight separates the hobbyist (5 or more, for steering speed) from the launcher (1.2–1.5, because gravity loss is $g\,t_b$ and the engines and structure are expensive).
  • Bigger is easier, up to a point: the square-cube law improves the structure fraction and drag with size.
  • A launcher is 85–92 % propellant, so its tanks are the rocket, and the density of the propellant sets the shape: LOX/kerosene ≈ 1 010 kg/m³, LOX/methane ≈ 830, LOX/hydrogen ≈ 350. Hydrogen’s tank is nearly three times the oxygen tank although the oxygen is five to six times heavier, because liquid hydrogen is about 16 times lighter than liquid oxygen.
  • Applying the rocket equation burn by burn to rounded public masses gives 9.0–9.5 km/s for the Shuttle, Saturn V, Soyuz, Falcon 9 and Starship, matching the ≈ 9.4 km/s orbit needs. One stage cannot do it unless its structure fraction is below $e^{-\Delta v/(I_{sp}g_0)}$: about 5.5 % with kerosene, 10 % with hydrogen.
  • A jet holds $F/g$ straight up (the CJ-1: 5.9 kg) but $F\,(L/D)/g$ sideways (≈ 59 kg at $L/D=10$): a wing multiplies the thrust by the lift-to-drag ratio. Height removes thrust (a third to a half of it at 8–10 km for the CJ-1), so aircraft have ceilings; a rocket’s thrust does not lapse, but it burns 7–30 times the propellant per newton.
