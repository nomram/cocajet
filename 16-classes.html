Chapter 16 · Classes of rockets

The rocket zoo: what the classes are

~45 min3 widgetsfrom a water bottle to Starship

A water rocket and a moon rocket obey the same equations, but their lift-off masses differ by a factor of ten million, and so does almost everything else about them: who may fly them, what motors they use, what they can carry and what they cost. People therefore sort rockets in several ways at once. This chapter lays out the sorting systems one by one (by motor size, by the rules, by job, by payload, by engine and architecture) and shows the one physical quantity, the thrust-to-weight ratio, that quietly decides which class a rocket must belong to.

Sort by motor size: the letters A to O

Chapter 13 introduced the label on a hobby motor. The letter is the total impulse, $I=\int F\,dt$, in newton-seconds, and each letter allows twice the impulse of the one before. That makes the ladder logarithmic, like the Richter scale: from an A motor to an O motor is fourteen doublings, a factor of more than 16 000.

For reference: the CJ-1 at full power for one minute delivers 3 500 N·s, an L-class impulse, but it delivers it as 58 N for 60 s instead of 1 000 N for 3 s. The class tells you the energy-like total, not how hard the push is; the second number in the label (the average thrust) and the burn time tell the rest. Notice what the ladder buys you: the Level system is a training path. To fly H and I motors you first prove you can build, fly and recover a rocket on smaller ones; to move up to J, K and L you do it again; and so on. It is the same logic as an aircraft licence, and the reason the hobby has an excellent safety record under clubs and a poor one outside them.

Staying on the right side of the lineThe categories exist because the hazard rises steeply with impulse: the energy in an O motor is thousands of times that in a C motor, and the distance at which a failure can hurt someone grows with it. Use certified motors from a seller who checks your certification, follow the club’s distance tables, and never modify a motor or build propellant at home (chapter 13). This chapter says nothing about weapons; military missiles share the physics but are outside this guide.

The classes are rough: the same rocket changes class depending on how much it saves for recovery, and the numbers shift between versions. The point is the pattern. Look at the chart and notice that the class boundaries line up with lift-off mass (a medium launcher weighs hundreds of tonnes, a super-heavy one thousands), and that the whole zoo falls along a band of constant thrust-to-weight ratio between 1.2 and 1.5, far below the hobby rockets.

Inside the giants: how big the tanks are

Sort by where it goes: the Δv budget

Chapter 12’s rocket equation says that a rocket is defined, above all, by how much velocity change $\Delta v$ it can deliver. Here is what each class of destination asks of the rocket, with the usual round numbers (they include gravity and drag losses where relevant):

Orbit is the expensive one: 9.4 km/s, three times the rest of the budget to the Moon put together. That is why launch vehicles are the big, multi-stage, high-class machines and why spacecraft, once in orbit, can use tiny engines.

The hidden boundary: thrust-to-weight ratio

There is one number that separates the hobby rocket from the orbital launcher, and it is not size: it is the lift-off thrust-to-weight ratio $T/W=F/(mg)$. A rocket accelerates at $(T/W-1)\,g$; at $T/W=1$ it does not even leave the pad. Why would a launcher not simply use a higher $T/W$? Look at the burn time for a fixed mass ratio $R$ and specific impulse $I_{sp}$:

$$t_b=\frac{(1-1/R)\,I_{sp}}{T/W},\qquad \Delta v_{\text{gravity}}\approx g\,t_b$$

The longer the burn, the longer gravity pulls the rocket back. With $I_{sp}=300$ s and $R=4$: at $T/W=1.3$ the burn takes 173 s and gravity eats 1.7 km/s; at $T/W=5$ it is 45 s and 440 m/s; at 15, 150 m/s. So a bigger $T/W$ is better for the speed, and the price is paid elsewhere: more engines, stronger structure, and more drag low in the dense air (the rocket is fastest when the air is thickest). The launchers’ compromise is $T/W\approx1.2$–$1.5$, and they then tilt over (a “gravity turn”) so that later in the climb gravity works against the horizontal velocity less.

A hobby rocket sits at the other end for a different reason. It has no guidance, only fins, and fins steer only if the air flows past them quickly. Clubs therefore ask for a thrust-to-weight of about 5 or more at lift-off and a speed of at least about 15 m/s leaving the launch rod. Why a big rocket is easier than a small one

Compare a model rocket and a moon rocket and you find one of the oldest rules in engineering, the square-cube law. If you scale a rocket up by a factor $L$ in every dimension, its propellant volume (and mass) grows as $L^3$ but its tank surface, and so the mass of a thin tank wall, grows only as $L^2$. The structure fraction $\varepsilon$ falls as the rocket grows, which, through the rocket equation’s logarithm, gives a better mass ratio for the same technology. Drag works the same way: the ballistic coefficient $m/(C_dA)$ grows as $L$, so a large rocket loses far less of its speed to the air than a small one. A hobby rocket can lose a quarter of its speed to drag; a launcher usually loses only a few hundred metres per second out of 9 400. The catch is that big rockets also have bigger problems (many engines in a small space, bigger tanks to cool and to keep from buckling) so each class needs its own engineering, not just a magnification.

What you now know

  • Motor classes A–O are letters for total impulse; each letter doubles the one before, and the second number in the label is the average thrust.
  • Rules follow the letters: model rockets (up to G, 160 N·s, up to 125 g of propellant, up to 1 500 g in total in the US); high-power H–O with club certification Levels 1–3 (H–I, J–L, M–O); anything above 40 960 N·s is a waiver-level “Class 3”.
  • By job: sounding rockets (suborbital), orbital launchers, upper stages, spacecraft thrusters.
  • Launch vehicles are classed by payload to LEO: small up to 2 t, medium 2–20 t, heavy 20–50 t, super-heavy above 50 t.
  • Δv sets the class: 1.5–2 km/s to the edge of space, 9.4 km/s to low orbit, then about 3 km/s more for the Moon, geostationary orbit or escape.
  • Thrust-to-weight separates the hobbyist (5 or more, for steering speed) from the launcher (1.2–1.5, because gravity loss is $g\,t_b$ and the engines and structure are expensive).
  • Bigger is easier, up to a point: the square-cube law improves the structure fraction and drag with size.
