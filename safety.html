Safety first

~15 min4 widgetspre-run checklist

A micro gas turbine is a 100 000 rpm flywheel with a blowtorch inside it, a noise like a jet fighter, and a 400 m/s jet out the back. Hobbyists build and fly them safely every day. The ones who stay safe respect these five facts.

This guide is educational

Everything in this guide (the physics, the maths, the STL files, the simulator) is meant to explain how a gas turbine works. It is not a build-safe, certified design and it has not been tested as hardware. Building a real one means machining rotating parts to ±0.05 mm, balancing them, and accepting real risks. If you don’t have access to a machine shop, an experienced mentor and a safe place to test, don’t build it: buy a commercial micro-turbine (they exist, with certified wheels, controllers and fail-safes) or just enjoy the simulator.

1 · Rotating energy: the wheel can burst

At 115 000 rpm the tips of our turbine blades move at 360 m/s: faster than sound and about as fast as a pistol bullet. Centrifugal stress goes as $\rho\omega^2r^2$: spin 10 % too fast and the stress rises 21 %. A cracked blade, an overheated disc, or a wheel out of balance can fail in a fraction of a second, and the pieces leave in the plane of the wheel at rim speed.

  • Never stand, sit, or let anyone stand in the plane of the compressor or turbine wheel (±15°).
  • Fit a containment ring (a thick steel band around the turbine) if you can; it is what separates a bang from a hazard.
  • Balance every wheel. A 1 g·mm imbalance at 115 000 rpm is 145 N of rotating force.
  • Never exceed the design speed. Fit a hard rpm limit in the controller.
  • Use proper alloys (310 stainless, Inconel) for the turbine wheel: no aluminium, no mild steel, no 3D-printed plastic, no mystery metal. See chapter 9.

2 · Fire and fuel

Propane is heavier than air: a leak pools in low spots and ignites on the first spark. At full power our engine burns 10 kg of it per hour; the hardware reaches 800 °C and the exhaust is hotter still. A wet start (too much fuel, no ignition) fills the combustor with gas that then goes off with a bang.

  • Outdoors, always. Never in a garage, shed, or under a roof. Exhaust contains carbon monoxide and, in an enclosed space, the engine will also exhaust all the oxygen.
  • Fail-closed solenoid, a regulator, a flashback arrestor, soapy-water leak tests, bottle upright and 3 m behind the engine.
  • A fire extinguisher (CO₂ or dry powder) and a second person.
  • If the engine does not light, close the gas, keep spinning the starter, and wait before trying again, so the unburned propane is blown out.

3 · Hot exhaust and the jet

The exhaust leaves at 377 m/s and about 670 °C. A jet slows with distance as roughly $V\approx6.3\,D\,V_e/x$, so a 40 mm jet is still 30 m/s (a storm) at 3 m. The casings glow dull-red in the dark and stay hot for many minutes after shut-down. Run the zones below to see how far the hazards reach.

4 · Noise

A small jet at full power measures 115–125 dB at 1 m. That is above the level at which hearing damage starts in seconds, and it is loud even half a kilometre away (neighbours, police, the dog). Plugs and muffs, every time, and a short run time:

5 · Intake, burns, fumes

  • Intake: only within about 15 cm of the bellmouth is the suction strong enough to pull a rag, a glove or hair. Keep loose clothing, jewellery and tools away; no gloves when near it. Put a mesh guard over the inlet.
  • Burns: the casing, flanges and exhaust remain above 60 °C (a burn threshold) for a long time after shut-down. Treat everything as hot until you’ve checked with a thermometer.
  • Fumes: carbon monoxide is odourless. Never run it indoors, and don’t sit downwind in the plume.
  • Heat soak: never shut off hot and just walk away: run the starter to cool the hot section, or the bearings, oil and casing heat-soak and fail.

A sensible test cell

ItemRecommendation
LocationOpen field, concrete pad or gravel; no grass; clear of buildings, roads, vehicles and people by 30 m in the jet line and the wheel planes.
Operator positionBehind a plywood-and-steel barrier, off-axis, 5+ m away, with the engine controlled remotely (cable or wireless) and a hardwired fuel-cut switch.
BarriersA thick steel plate or sand-filled barrels between the engine’s wheel planes and any people.
Thrust standBolted to a heavy base; thrust measured with a load cell, never by hand.
SensorsEGT, rpm, fuel pressure; automatic cut-out above set EGT/rpm limits.
PeopleTwo people minimum; briefed; hearing and eye protection; spectators far away.

Legal and neighbours

Rules vary by country and city, and this page is not legal advice. Check: noise ordinances; storage and handling of LPG/propane (quantity limits, bottle certification); open-flame and burn bans; any model-aircraft rules if you plan to fly it (jet-powered models are often restricted to approved fields and certified pilots); and insurance (a model-flying club’s policy may cover turbines; your home policy probably doesn’t). Telling your neighbours before a test run is both polite and sensible.

The pre-run checklist

Bottom lineIf a safe run requires you to relax any item above, don’t run. A home-built turbine that has been designed, machined, balanced, tested and operated by someone who respects it is a fine hobby. One built carelessly is a bomb with a gearbox. Use the simulator to learn the sequence; use the checklist; ask experienced builders (the model-turbine community is generous with advice).
