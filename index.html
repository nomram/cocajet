The whole idea in four words

A jet engine is an advanced hair-dryer with a flame in it. It does four things, over and over, without stopping:

1 · SUCK

Take in air

A bellmouth gathers a big, slow stream of ordinary air: ~78% nitrogen, ~21% oxygen.

2 · SQUEEZE

Compress it

A spinning wheel throws the air outward, packing it to twice atmospheric pressure. Squeezing heats it by ~90 °C.

3 · BANG

Burn fuel in it

Fuel burns continuously inside a can. The heat makes the gas want to expand. Only ¼ of the air is used to burn; the rest cools the flame.

4 · BLOW

Let it escape

The hot gas spins a turbine (which drives the compressor), then rushes out the back. Action, reaction: the engine is pushed forward.

That is the Brayton cycle. Everything in this guide is a closer look at one of those four steps, and at the physics (Newton, gas laws, aerodynamics, chemistry, materials) that makes each step work.

What is “the Coke-can jet motor”?

Hobbyists have long built tiny gas turbines out of scrap parts. One famous trick is using a soda can as the flame tube: the thin aluminium sheet is easy to cut and drill, and the cooling air that streams through its holes keeps it alive long enough to learn from. Our design (call it CJ-1) uses exactly that idea inside a proper machined structure: an annular flame tube made from a Coke can wrapped around a central shaft tunnel. The honest catch: aluminium melts at 660 °C, so the can is a consumable teaching liner. Chapter 9 shows what to swap it for.
