Frequently asked questions

Can I really build this?

It is a serious hobby project: it needs a lathe, welding, access to balancing, a safe place to test and ideally someone experienced to look over your shoulder. Many people have built working micro-turbines from turbochargers; far fewer have done so without an accident or a scrapped wheel. This guide teaches how and why the engine works, and its design is educational rather than proven. If you only want to see one, the simulator and the 3D viewer are the safe way.

Why propane and not kerosene?

Gas mixes with air with no effort, so the combustor can be a simple ring of fuel lances. Kerosene (Jet-A, diesel) needs a vaporiser or fine atomiser and a pump, which is another part to design and keep alive. Propane also burns cleanly and stores safely in a bottle. The disadvantage is that it is heavier than air and pools in low spots when it leaks: see the safety page.

Why is the pressure ratio only about 2:1?

A single, small, aluminium centrifugal wheel runs out of tip speed and strength. 337 m/s at the tip is already near Mach 0.9 relative to the air in the inducer. Higher pressure needs a stronger wheel, more compressor stages, or both: that is what airliners do, up to 40:1.

Why does the engine need a starter?

At low speed the compressor squeezes so little air that the turbine can only break even with a very hot flame, so the engine cannot sustain itself. The starter spins the rotor up to about 17 000 rpm, we light the fuel, and the starter keeps helping until the engine is self-sustaining at roughly 45 000 rpm (idle is 48 000). In the simulator you can watch this happen and see what a hot start or a wet start does.

Can I print the STL files and run the engine?

No. A printed compressor wheel would shatter; a printed turbine wheel would melt. Printing is for fit-checks, patterns for casting, jigs and gauges. The gallery page says what each part must really be made from.

How accurate is the simulator?

It is a physics model with simplifications: a one-dimensional mean-line compressor, a lumped-heat metal model, a single-zone combustor. It captures trends and orders of magnitude (thrust, temperatures, the start-up sequence, how things go wrong), not the last 10 %. Real engines differ with machining quality, leaks, ambient conditions and many things not modelled. Treat it as a training aid, not as a certificate.

Can I make my own rocket propellant or motor?

This guide deliberately does not teach that. Chapters 12–15 explain how rockets work, in the way a university textbook does: physics, chemistry, stability and failure modes. They give no formulations and no manufacturing steps. Propellant and motor manufacture is regulated in almost every country, depends on tests that a home workshop cannot do (rate tests, proof pressure, X-ray inspection) and is a leading cause of injuries in rocketry. The safe routes are a water rocket (chapter 12) and certified motors flown through a club (NAR, Tripoli, UKRA or your national equivalent).

Why not simply put an electric fan on it instead of a turbine?

Chapter 15 does that comparison: a ducted fan turns electricity into thrust 27 times more efficiently than the CJ-1 turns fuel heat into thrust, but a lithium battery holds about 70 times less energy per kilogram than propane. For a five-minute run the propane jet is still lighter overall; for hobby jets that fly short sorties, the electric fan wins on simplicity and noise.

Are the ion-wind and spark-plug projects safe to try at home?

Not without supervision. Ion wind needs 20–40 kV (a shock hazard even at milliamps) and chapter 15 is a calculator, not a build guide. The home-made spark plug of chapter 11 is a low-power igniter for a gas burner in a test cell; read that chapter’s safety notes first and use the 3D-printed parts only as patterns and fit-checks.

Does the site work offline?

Yes: the 3D library, math renderer and all STL files are included in the repository (no external CDNs). Because the pages load JavaScript modules and STL files, open them through a local web server (python3 -m http.server in the project folder) rather than by double-clicking, since browsers block these requests from file:// pages.

How loud is it, really?

115–125 dB at 1 m at full power: comparable to a jet-ski or a chainsaw, but with a shriek that carries. Hearing damage starts in seconds without protection. The safety page has a calculator.

Can I use these materials and numbers in my own design?

Use them to learn and to estimate, not as design allowables. Material curves are rounded, representative values; real alloys vary by supplier, heat treatment and batch. A real design needs the data sheet of the actual material and a safety factor you can justify.

Sources and further reading

The equations here are standard textbook thermodynamics and turbomachinery. The numbers (efficiencies, losses, material curves) are typical, rounded values for small engines, chosen so the whole guide agrees with itself; they are not measurements of a specific engine. For anything you rely on, check them against these sources.

MODEL TURBINES

Schreckling, Gas Turbine Engines for Model Aircraft

Traplet Publications, 1994. The classic builder’s book: design, parts, starting and running of hobby turbines.

TEXTBOOK

Saravanamuttoo, Rogers, Cohen, Straznicky, Nix: Gas Turbine Theory

Pearson, 7th ed. 2017. Cycles, components, off-design matching: the backbone of chapters 3 and 5.

TEXTBOOK

Mattingly, Elements of Propulsion: Gas Turbines and Rockets

AIAA, 2nd ed. 2006. Thrust equation, cycle analysis, afterburners, bypass engines.

TURBOMACHINERY

Dixon & Hall, Fluid Mechanics and Thermodynamics of Turbomachinery

Butterworth-Heinemann, 7th ed. 2014. Velocity triangles, Euler work, centrifugal compressors, radial and axial turbines.

COMBUSTION

Lefebvre & Ballal, Gas Turbine Combustion

CRC Press, 3rd ed. 2010. Primary / secondary / dilution zones, liner cooling, stability limits.

AERODYNAMICS

Anderson, Fundamentals of Aerodynamics

McGraw-Hill. Lift, angle of attack, stall, compressible flow and nozzles.

OVERVIEW

Rolls-Royce, The Jet Engine

Wiley, 5th ed. 2015. An illustrated introduction to every part of a real engine.

ENGINEERING

Boyce, Gas Turbine Engineering Handbook

Elsevier. Practical operation, bearings, vibration, materials and failure modes.

PAPER

Wiesner, F. J. (1967)

“A review of slip factors for centrifugal impellers”, Journal of Engineering for Power 89(4), 558–572. The slip formula in chapter 4.

Larson & Miller (1952)

“A time-temperature relationship for rupture and creep stresses”, Transactions of the ASME 74, 765–775. The creep parameter in chapter 9.

“Das Maximum der theoretisch möglichen Ausnützung des Windes durch Windmotoren”, Zeitschrift für das gesamte Turbinenwesen 26. The 16/27 limit.

ISO 2533 · ISO 21940-11 · EN 1993-1-2 / 1-4

COMBUSTION

Turns, An Introduction to Combustion
