Materials science: why the can melts and what doesn’t

~40 min5 widgetsnumbers you can check

The thermodynamics of chapter 3 says: hotter inlet, more thrust. The reason a cheap engine doesn’t run hotter is not the compressor or the fuel: it is that every metal we can afford gets weak, stretches and oxidises long before the flame gets uncomfortable.

Four enemies of a hot, spinning part

1 · STRENGTH

Yield drops with heat

Most metals lose half their strength by 500–700 °C; aluminium by 250 °C.

2 · CREEP

Slow stretching

Hot metal under constant load flows, even far below its yield, until it ruptures.

3 · OXIDATION

Burning slowly

Hot air eats the surface (scale). Chromium and nickel alloys grow a protective skin.

4 · FATIGUE

Cycles & vibration

Every start-up, every blade resonance, every thermal cycle adds a tiny crack.

Strength versus temperature

Tick the metals to compare. Move the red stress line and the temperature to read off safety factors. Try 190 MPa at 750 °C (the turbine blade root) and then try 5 MPa at 600 °C (a flame-tube wall).

Two things jump out: aluminium (can and 6061) collapses by 200–300 °C, so it can only live where cooled; stainless 304 and 310 hold a modest 100 MPa at 800 °C (not enough for a spinning blade); and the nickel alloys keep several hundred MPa to 800 °C.

Stress in the spinning rotor

A blade at radius $r$ is pulled outward by $m\omega^2r$. Add up the mass beyond each point and the root of a blade carries $\sigma\approx\tfrac12\rho\omega^2(r_t^2-r_h^2)$: it depends on the material density (heavy nickel alloys hurt) and on the square of the speed. A disc is loaded by its own mass (peak stress at the bore) plus the pull of the blades on its rim.

What this says about CJ-1, honestly
  • Turbine wheel at 115 000 rpm, ~750 °C: blade root ≈ 250 MPa. Inconel 718 (yield ~720 MPa hot) carries the blade root with a safety factor of 2.8; the disc bore is the limit, at 1.65 on yield and a burst margin of ~1.4× in speed: tight but proper. Stainless 310S (yield ~120 MPa hot) is far over its limit. Even 625 (300 MPa) is marginal. The “310 for a hobby build” advice you might read elsewhere applies to far slower, cooler wheels.
  • Compressor wheel: disc stress 130–250 MPa vs 6061’s ~240 MPa at 120 °C: no margin. Forged 2618 is the right alloy, and a scrap turbo wheel is already proven.
  • Either way a speed limiter and a containment ring are not optional.

Creep: the slow failure

Yield tells you if a part gives way now. At high temperature a part under a steady load also gives way later: it creeps. The usual way to compare alloys is the Larson–Miller parameter $\text{LMP}=T\,(C+\log_{10}t)$ with $T$ in kelvin, $t$ in hours and $C\approx20$. For a given stress, LMP is a constant of the alloy, so you can trade time for temperature.

The 4× life change per 25 °C is why the turbine inlet temperature is controlled so tightly on real engines, and why a hot start (EGT 100 °C too high for a few seconds) uses up a chunk of the blade’s life.

Thermal expansion and thermal stress

Heat a metal bar by ΔT and it grows by $\Delta L=\alpha L\,\Delta T$ (steel $\alpha\approx12$, stainless 16–17, aluminium 23 µm/m/K). If you stop it from growing, it pushes back with stress $\sigma=E\alpha\Delta T$: for steel that is 2.5 MPa per kelvin. A thin wall heated on one face and cold on the other (a start-up transient) feels about half of that.

Design ruleEvery hot–cold joint in the engine is a floating joint: the flame tube rests on its dome with a loose fit, the NGV ring slides inside the turbine casing, the nozzle flange is bolted with slotted holes. Rigidly bolting a hot part to a cold one makes it buckle, crack at the weld or loosen after the first heat cycle.

Why the Coke can survives (for a while)

A flame tube sees a 2 000 K flame inside and 366 K air outside, and aluminium melts at 650 °C. Its wall temperature is a heat balance: hot gas heats it by convection and radiation; cool air (outside, and the film along the inside) carries the heat away. The knob is the cooling-film effectiveness: how much of the cooling air actually covers the wall.

With a good film (η ≈ 0.5) the can sits just below its melting point: that’s why it lasts minutes but not hours. With η ≥ 0.6 or a stainless or Inconel wall, it lasts for hours or years. In the simulator, pick “Coke can” and run at full power; then pick “Stainless 310S”.

Oxidation, corrosion and fatigue

Hot air attacks metal. Iron alone forms a thick flaky scale that spalls off; chromium (and nickel) form a thin, sticky oxide skin that protects the metal underneath. That is what “stainless” and “Inconel” mean in practice. Typical maximum continuous service temperatures in air (approximate):

Fatigue is the other quiet killer: a blade is excited at multiples of the rotation speed (once per vane passing, 16× per rev for our NGV). If a blade’s natural frequency coincides with one of these “engine orders” in the operating range, it resonates and cracks. Real designs plot a Campbell diagram; hobby builders avoid it by (a) stiff, short blades, (b) a prime blade count (19) against an even vane count (16), and (c) passing quickly through any resonance.

What you now know

  • Metals lose strength with temperature; aluminium goes at 250 °C, steel/stainless at 600–800 °C, nickel alloys keep their strength to ~800 °C.
  • Centrifugal stress goes as ρ(ωr)²: spinning parts need strong, hot-strong alloys; our turbine blade root sees ~250 MPa at ~750 °C: Inconel only.
  • Creep life drops about 4× per 25 °C; one overtemperature start can eat a chunk of the blade’s life.
  • Thermal expansion means every hot–cold joint must float.
  • The Coke can lives on its film of cooling air; it is a teaching liner, not a long-life one.
