Chapter 13 · Solid rockets

Solid rockets: fuel and air in one block

~50 min2 widgetsconcept level: no recipes

The oldest rocket in the world has no pump, no valve and no moving part: a hollow tube of solid propellant with a nozzle at one end. From Chinese fire-arrows to the boosters of the Space Shuttle, the design is the same, and so is its one big drawback: once lit, you cannot turn it off. This chapter explains how a solid motor sets its own pressure, why a single exponent decides whether it is safe or a bomb, how the shape of the block shapes the thrust, and how to read the label on a model-rocket motor.

Scope of this chapterEverything here is physics and engineering at the level of a university textbook. It deliberately contains no propellant formulations, no mixing ratios and no manufacturing steps, and the widgets work with generic “burn-rate numbers”, not with ingredients. Propellants and motors are made safely only in licensed plants, are regulated in almost every country, and amateur manufacture is a leading cause of injuries in rocketry. If you want to fly solid rockets, use certified motors through a club (see the end of the chapter).

A fuel that brings its own oxygen

Chapter 10 showed that a fuel releases energy only when it meets an oxidiser, and that air is a free supply of one. A solid propellant packs both into one block: an oxidiser, a fuel (usually a rubbery binder that also holds everything together, often with a metal powder as extra fuel) and small amounts of additives, blended so intimately that they react layer by layer from the exposed surface inwards. It burns without air, so it works in a vacuum.

It does burn, it does not detonate. This is the difference you met in chapter 10: a burning front crawls into the unburnt material at millimetres per second, driven by heat conduction and pressure, while a detonation is a shock wave moving at kilometres per second. A solid motor is a pressure vessel designed to contain a controlled deflagration; every failure mode in this chapter is some way of turning the first into the second, or of letting the pressure win.

Families of solid propellant (and what each ingredient does)

Solid propellants come in three broad families. The table describes the roles and trade-offs, never the amounts.

What does each part of a composite do?

  • The oxidiser (the crystalline salt ammonium perchlorate is the standard one) supplies the oxygen. It is by far the bulk of the mass. Its chlorine ends up as hydrogen chloride in the exhaust: that is the white cloud behind a big booster, acidic and an environmental concern, and one reason the material is regulated.
  • The binder is a rubbery polymer that is both the main fuel and the glue: it gives the block mechanical strength and elasticity so it survives storage, vibration and acceleration without cracking.
  • The metal fuel (powdered aluminium) raises the flame temperature a lot (chapter 10: energy per kilogram) and the aluminium oxide it leaves is a white smoke of hot solid particles. The particles lose some efficiency, because a solid particle does not expand like a gas, and they are what makes the exhaust so bright.
  • Burn-rate modifiers in tiny amounts speed the burn; the size of the oxidiser crystals does too. Chapter 10’s particle-size widget showed why: finer particles expose more surface, so the reaction zone is faster. Designers blend coarse and fine to tune the rate.

Real compositions are proprietary, tuned over decades and tested batch by batch; we leave them out on purpose. One more reason is worth stating plainly. Fine powders are a dust-explosion and a static-ignition hazard; the minimum ignition energy of chapter 11, which for a flammable powder can be as low as a few millijoules, is less than the spark from your sleeve. That is why propellants are made in remote, grounded, remotely operated plants. “Home-made propellant” is a recurring cause of burns, lost hands and criminal records, and the tests that separate a safe motor from a bomb (strand-burner rate tests, proof pressure, X-ray inspection of every grain) are exactly what a kitchen lacks.

How fast does it burn? r = a·Pⁿ

The burning surface recedes at a speed $r$ that rises with the pressure of the gas pressing on it: more pressure pushes the flame closer to the surface, so more heat flows back to the solid. Measured over a wide range, the result is the empirical burn-rate law:

$$r=a\,P_c^{\,n}\qquad\text{e.g. }a=4\ \text{mm/s at 1 MPa},\ n=0.35\ \Rightarrow\ r\approx6\ \text{mm/s at 3.4 MPa}$$

The coefficient $a$ depends on the propellant and on the grain temperature: a cold grain burns slower (lower pressure, less thrust), a hot one faster. The reason is chapter 10’s Arrhenius law again, since the surface reaction runs faster when the solid is warmer. A motor is therefore certified for a temperature range, and a rocket left in a hot car is not a rocket to fire. The pressure exponent $n$ is the star of this chapter: it says how sensitive the burn is to pressure. Typical values are 0.2 to 0.6 for composites; some special “plateau” propellants reach nearly zero.

Pressure finds its own level

Take the chamber as a box. Propellant turns into gas at the rate $\dot m_{gen}=\rho A_b\,r$ (density times burning area times the speed of the surface). The throat lets it out at $\dot m_{out}=P_cA_t/c^*$ (the choked-nozzle result of chapter 12, with $c^*\approx1\,400$ m/s). If more is made than leaves, the pressure rises, which makes the grain burn faster and the gas leave faster. The two balance when

$$\rho A_b\,aP_c^{\,n}=\frac{P_cA_t}{c^*}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P_c=\big(\rho\,c^*a\,K_n\big)^{\frac1{1-n}},\qquad K_n=\frac{A_b}{A_t}$$

Worked example (the default motor in the widget below): grain 29 mm across with a 9 mm hole, two 50 mm segments, throat 5.5 mm, so $A_b=5\,200$ mm², $A_t=23.8$ mm² and $K_n=219$. With $\rho=1\,750$ kg/m³, $c^*=1\,430$ m/s, $a=4$ mm/s at 1 MPa and $n=0.35$ (in units of mm/s and MPa the bracket is $\rho c^*aK_n/10^9=2.19$): $P_c=2.19^{1/0.65}=3.35$ MPa, about 33 atmospheres. The nozzle ($\varepsilon=4$, $C_F=1.49$) then gives $F=C_FP_cA_t=119$ N, $I_{sp}=C_Fc^*/g_0=218$ s, and the 10 mm web burns through in $10/6.1\approx1.6$ s: total impulse ≈ 200 N·s, a class H motor.

Notice how the pressure responds to the area. Differentiate: $\dfrac{dP_c}{P_c}=\dfrac{1}{1-n}\dfrac{dK_n}{K_n}$.

Shaping the thrust: the grain

The burning area $A_b$ changes as the surface recedes, so the shape of the hole is the throttle. The design problem is to choose a shape whose area follows the thrust you want:

  • Neutral (constant area, constant thrust): an end burner that burns like a cigarette (long, thin, and the whole case sees the flame), or a tube burning inside and out where the inner surface grows exactly as fast as the outer one shrinks.
  • Progressive (rising thrust): a plain bore burner: the hole grows, so the area grows. Used when you want to push harder as the rocket gets lighter.
  • Regressive (falling thrust): an outside-burning rod. Useful for limiting acceleration late in the burn.
  • BATES grains (bore plus both end faces burning; stacked in segments): the hobby standard because the segments are simply cast or pressed and stacked, and the thrust stays nearly constant.
  • Star, wagon-wheel, finocyl, moon-burner grains: more complex perforations that give a high initial thrust, a plateau and a clean tail. The Space Shuttle booster’s forward segment had a star-shaped hole so that the thrust dipped as the vehicle passed through the most aerodynamically stressful minute of the flight.

Try them. Choose a shape, change the throat, and watch the grain regress in the cut-away; click the picture or press “Fire” to scrub through the burn. The rest of the readouts are what a motor designer checks first: the designation, the pressure against the case, and what is left over.

Four things to try in the widget

  1. Pick each grain in turn (the throat re-sizes itself so the largest $K_n$ of the burn is 265). Bore burner: thrust rises. Rod: it falls. Tube: flat. BATES: a gentle hump, since its 10 mm web is thin compared with the segment length.
  2. Shrink the throat by 20 % (5.5 → 4.4 mm): the area ratio rises by 56 % and the pressure almost doubles, so the burst margin falls to 1.4. Open the throat instead (to 10 mm or more): the pressure sinks below the self-sustaining limit and the fire goes out part-way through, leaving most of the grain unburnt (a motor that “chuffs”).
  3. Raise the exponent $n$ from 0.35 to 0.7 with the same throat: the equilibrium pressure is four times higher, and the 12 MPa case bursts within 20 ms. This is the stability widget in action.
  4. Add a hidden crack that doubles the burning area (100 %): the extra surface exists from the first instant, and the pressure nearly reaches the case limit; at n = 0.5 it exceeds it. That is the textbook CATO (“catastrophic at take-off”), the most common way a motor fails.

Second-order effects the model includes (and some it hides)

  • Erosive burning. If gas races down a hole that is too narrow compared with the throat (a port-to-throat area ratio below about 2) the fast flow strips heat into the surface and the grain burns faster than the law says, giving a pressure spike right at ignition. Designers keep the port wide, or accept a longer, thinner motor. The toggle switches it on and off.
  • Slivers. The last bits of a grain are thin remnants that burn out while the pressure falls. Below about 0.7 MPa the propellant stops burning (the deflagration limit); the leftover is dead weight and sometimes thrown out unburnt.
  • Tail-off and ignition spike. The pressure takes a few milliseconds to climb and a few tens of milliseconds to fall; hobbyists call the slow part the “tail”.

Reading a model-rocket motor label

Certified model motors carry a code such as C6-5 or G79-10:

$$\underbrace{\text{C}}_{\text{total impulse class}}\ \underbrace{\text{6}}_{\text{average thrust (N)}}\ -\ \underbrace{\text{5}}_{\text{delay (s)}}$$

The letter is the total impulse $I=\int F\,dt$ in newton-seconds; each letter allows twice the impulse of the one before. The number is the average thrust over the burn, and the delay (in seconds) is the time between burn-out and the small ejection charge that pops the recovery system. Class A starts at about 1.25 N·s:

For scale: the CJ-1’s 58 N for one minute is 3 500 N·s, a class L. The turbojet would need 170 g of propane for that minute; the solid motor would need 1.6 kg of propellant (58 N at an Isp of 220 s). How solid motors fail and how professionals prevent it

FailurePhysicsDefence
Cracked or debonded grainthe flame reaches new surface: $A_b$ jumps; pressure rises like $1/(1-n)$ (above)mechanical design for thermal and acceleration strains; X-ray and ultrasonic inspection of every grain; no re-use, no modifications
Voids and porosityhidden holes burn open and give a pressure spikevacuum casting; inspection; batch testing

Case too weakhoop stress $\sigma=P_cr/t$ exceeds the strength (the 3.4 MPa, 20 mm radius, 2 mm wall example gives 34 MPa, well within aluminium, but no margin for a 3× pressure error)design for ≥ 1.5–2× the maximum expected pressure; hydrostatic proof test of each case
Joint or seal leakhot gas finds a small gap, erodes it, and the leak grows: the Challenger accident of 1986 was a failed rubber O-ring seal in a booster joint that had lost its elasticity in near-freezing weather, not a propellant faultredundant seals, temperature limits, joint redesign, flight-rules that forbid launching outside the tested temperature range
Wrong temperaturea hot grain burns faster, so pressure rises with the temperature coefficient of $a$ (a few tenths of a percent per kelvin)a stated temperature range for every motor
Accidental ignitionstatic spark, friction, heat: the minimum ignition energy of chapter 11grounded handling, no sparks, no heat, no mixing of fine powders outside licensed plants

Notice the common thread: every defence is applied before the fire is lit, because a solid motor cannot be shut down afterwards. A clean design, a tested batch, a verified case, and a cautious operator are the only safety system there is, which is why the hobby route and the professional route both insist on certified, factory-made motors.

The responsible way to fly solid rockets

  • Join a club: the National Association of Rocketry (USA), Tripoli Rocketry Association, UKRA (UK) and their national equivalents run launches with range-safety officers, certification levels for larger motors, insurance and a published safety code.
  • Use certified commercial motors exactly as supplied. Never cut, drill, re-pack or “improve” a motor, never use an old or damaged one, never use a motor above your certification level.
  • Launch electrically from a stand, from a distance, with a clear range and the club’s published minimum safe distances for each motor class.
  • Simulate before you fly: free flight-simulation programs (OpenRocket, RASAero) take a certified motor’s published thrust curve (thrustcurve.org collects them) and predict stability, apogee and landing speed; the thrust-curve and impulse widget above shows what such a curve is made of.
  • Know the law: in most countries buying, storing and transporting propellant and motors is regulated by class; ask the club before you buy anything.
The honest comparison with our jetThe CJ-1 is a rocket’s opposite in one respect: you can shut it off. Close the fuel valve and it is a cold turbine in a minute. A solid motor never gives you that option. Chapter 14 looks at the rockets that do: hybrids and liquids.

What you now know

  • A solid propellant is fuel and oxidiser in one block; it burns in parallel layers (millimetres per second), it does not detonate.
  • Burn rate $r=aP_c^n$; chamber pressure settles at $P_c=(\rho c^*aK_n)^{1/(1-n)}$ with $K_n=A_b/A_t$, and thrust $F=C_FP_cA_t$.
  • Stability needs $n<1$: the pressure is then self-correcting. The closer $n$ is to 1, the more a small area error (a crack, a blocked throat) is amplified: the factor is $1/(1-n)$. For $n\ge1$ the pressure runs away.

  • Grain shape sets the thrust curve: neutral, progressive or regressive.
  • Motor class letters double in total impulse; “C6-5” means class C, 6 N average, 5 s delay.
  • All safety is designed in before ignition: inspected grains, proven cases, tested batches, certified motors.
